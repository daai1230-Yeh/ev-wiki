# Study: E-retrofits could accelerate bus fleet electrification by 15 years

**Author**: Jasmin Apitzsch-Krause | **Date**: 2026-07-16

---

A study by the Swiss Federal Laboratories for Materials Science and Technology (Empa) highlights the potential of converting diesel buses to battery-electric drivetrains. Rather than waiting for vehicles to be replaced at the end of their service life, e-retrofitting could accelerate the electrification of Europe's bus fleet by around 15 years — which the researchers say could be crucial for meeting climate targets.

Europe's bus electrification is gathering pace but advancing more slowly than climate targets require. The bottleneck isn't the availability of battery-electric buses but the long service life of existing ones — city buses run for about 20 years on average. Even if only zero-emission buses were newly registered from 2035 onward, Europe's bus fleet wouldn't reach 95% electrification until 2057, per Empa's calculations.

E-retrofitting could help close that gap: instead of running diesel buses to the end of their life or replacing them early, operators can convert them to battery-electric drivetrains, keeping the body, chassis, and interior in service while replacing only the engine, gearbox, fuel tank, and exhaust system. Empa's life-cycle assessment finds retrofitting generates roughly half the greenhouse gas emissions of producing a new battery-electric bus — about 58 tonnes of CO₂-equivalent saved per vehicle. Applied across Europe's bus fleet, the approach could deliver around 300 million tonnes of CO₂ savings by 2100, roughly equivalent to Spain's annual emissions.

Economically, a retrofit costs roughly half as much as a new battery-electric bus, and combined with electric buses' lower operating costs, the study estimates e-retrofitting could cut total bus transport costs by 2.2% to 3.2% by 2100, with savings reinvestable into transit expansion. The approach could also ease pressure on manufacturers to ramp up new electric bus production as fast, giving them more time to expand capacity and supply chains, while creating new business for standardized retrofit kits, battery systems, and electric axles — and new work for workshops as traditional maintenance business declines.

Per the study, just 1% of today's automotive mechatronics workforce would be enough to retrofit up to 75,000 buses per year; allocating 5% of that workforce could theoretically electrify Europe's existing diesel bus fleet in under three years.

The findings aren't without caveats: the economic analysis rests on model assumptions and limited pilot data since large-scale retrofit programs remain uncommon, actual costs will vary by vehicle platform and battery concept, and questions around certification, warranties, and standardization are unresolved. The study also doesn't address charging infrastructure in depth — operators would still need investment in charging, grid connections, and energy management, with battery supply likely the main bottleneck.

Whether e-retrofitting gains real momentum, the authors conclude, will depend less on technical feasibility than on policy: funding programs today focus almost entirely on new electric bus purchases, and expanding retrofitting's role would require adapted funding schemes, standardized approval processes, and industrialized retrofit solutions.
