# VW ID. Polo customers now face year-long wait times

**Author**: Florian Treiss | **Date**: 2026-11-23

---

Volkswagen has apparently significantly underestimated the success of its new electric small car family. Customers for the VW ID. Polo sometimes have to wait more than a year, and the other three models also require a lot of patience. Now, production in Spain is to be scaled up more quickly. However, a shortage of battery cells is also slowing down production.

The VW ID. Polo has become an unexpected sales success for Volkswagen – while simultaneously presenting the group with major challenges in meeting demand. By the end of September, around 53,000 orders had already been placed for the electric compact car, as VW brand chief Thomas Schäfer revealed at the Paris Motor Show in October. The figure is likely to be significantly higher by now.

Dealers have reported waiting times of more than a year to Automobilwoche, up from the ten months mentioned in September. According to Spanish outlet Motor.es, delivery times currently vary considerably: while the cheapest Trend trim, starting at €24,995 in Germany, could take up to 21 months to deliver, more expensive variants like the Sportline may only require a five-month wait, and the GTI seven months.

## VW ID. Polo production capacity to rise to 1,200 cars

The high demand comes at an inopportune time for Volkswagen: production at the Martorell plant in Spain, where the ID. Polo and the Cupra Raval share an assembly line, still needs to be ramped up to full capacity. According to a VW spokesperson, the plant currently produces up to 1,000 electric cars per day. In the first half of 2027, production is set to gradually expand to three shifts and full capacity, which will then reach up to 1,200 vehicles per day or around 300,000 units annually.

A VW spokesperson told Automobilwoche: “Production capacities and the ramp-up of new models are planned long-term and cannot be adjusted short-term to the same extent as this dynamically increasing demand.” Demand has developed “significantly more dynamically than originally expected.”

In October, Seat-Cupra CEO Markus Haupt told Spanish media that the ramp-up would take time. The line was initially to be expanded to two and a half shifts, with daily production of around 930 vehicles. To transition to three shifts, supplier logistics must be secured in addition to workforce planning. For this, Seat has hired nearly 1,000 additional employees.

## Battery cell supply faces disruption

The strain on the supply situation became evident as early as October. Due to a shortage of battery cells, Seat had to temporarily halt production on the electric car line in Martorell. According to union representatives, a multi-day shutdown may have resulted in around 1,900 vehicles not being built. Spanish media also reported further interruptions in the following weeks and issues with transporting battery cells from China.

Yet Martorell does have a high-performance battery assembly facility. The plant can produce up to 1,200 battery systems per day. However, the cells themselves come from external suppliers such as Gotion. The new gigafactory of VW’s battery subsidiary PowerCo in Sagunto, Spain, is also unlikely to provide immediate relief: pre-series production was scheduled to begin there in autumn, with stable series production planned for the first half of 2027.

## Delivery issues also affect Cupra Raval

The VW ID. Polo is not the only model exceeding its original production forecast. The Cupra Raval has also received more than 52,000 orders. Together, the two models have already accumulated over 100,000 orders.

For all four models of the Electric Urban Car Family – VW ID. Polo, Cupra Raval, VW ID. Cross, and Škoda Epiq – Volkswagen had recorded around 150,000 orders by the end of September.

The two compact electric SUVs are produced at the Pamplona plant, though they share the same assembly line as the internal combustion models T-Cross and Taigo. For 2027, Volkswagen plans a total production of 360,000 vehicles there. The Škoda Epiq is also in high demand: Spanish media report more than 80,000 orders and waiting times of up to 17 months in Germany.

## Electric compact car family likely to remain a challenge for Volkswagen

Volkswagen must now weigh how quickly capacities can be expanded and which models should take priority. In Martorell, increasing ID. Polo production without changing utilisation would come at the expense of the Cupra Raval. Additional production lines would require significant investment and several months of lead time. Furthermore, short-term follow-up orders with suppliers could drive up procurement costs. The success of the new electric compact car family thus presents Volkswagen with challenges in production, supply chains, and profitability.

Volkswagen is not the only manufacturer caught off guard by the high demand for electric cars. The German Association of Automobile Dealers (VAD) states that more than 40 per cent of new car orders currently involve electric vehicles. Depending on the manufacturer, this comes with delivery times of three to six months, but in some cases more than a year. “Our customers sometimes have to wait extremely long for their electric cars and are rightfully frustrated,” VAD president Burkhard Weller has said. The already high registration figures for electric cars, with a recent 37 per cent market share (DE), only reflect the trend with a delay, according to the VAD, because many electric car orders will not be delivered and registered until next year.
