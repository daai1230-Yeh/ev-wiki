# 'Mandates drive scale': ICCT India's Amit Bhatt calls for a policy-led EV push

**Author**: Sagar Parikh | **Date**: 2026-09-09

---

India's EV transition has gathered significant momentum in recent years, although adoption remains uneven across vehicle segments. At the India Clean Transportation Summit 2026 in New Delhi, we spoke with Amit Bhatt, Managing Director for India at the ICCT, about the country's progress and policy direction.

The Indian government has introduced several schemes and various other measures to directly and indirectly promote EV adoption. While it is focusing mainly on battery-electric powertrains, it continues to experiment with other alternative technologies, including hydrogen fuel-cell and combustion powertrains, as it explores different routes to decarbonise transport.

At the India Clean Transportation Summit 2026 in New Delhi, we spoke with ICCT's Managing Director for India, Amit Bhatt, to get an overview of the general progress of electrification in the country, his assessment of related government initiatives, and his outlook for the road ahead.

**Looking at India's present rate of EV adoption, do you believe the country is on course to meet its ambitious target of 30% EV penetration by 2030? Which segments are progressing adequately, and which are falling behind?**

So, 30% by 2030 is actually not an official target. It's been spoken about a lot. It's been in public discourse for a long time. If you look at where we were at the start of the year versus where we are now, there's a huge amount of change.

A lot of it has changed in the last six months because of the West Asia crisis. Right now, if you look at, we are almost 8% in terms of cars, which is a very strong figure. Two-wheelers, if you look at, I was speaking to one of the researchers, they're saying right now the average shelf life of an electric two-wheeler is four days. In four days, that vehicle is sold out.

And so, things have changed because, one, people have now realised that if you're dependent on oil, you'll always have to suffer these fluctuations. And second, people now have trust on technology much more than what it was before. And that's why I think a lot of change has happened.

So, we are doing good on two-wheelers. We are doing exceptionally well on three-wheelers. And I think one policy which has really made a big change is the Delhi EV Policy, which says that we'll stop registering ICE vehicles of certain categories, for three-wheelers from January 2027 and for two-wheelers from April 2028.

That really kind of made a huge dent on OEMs. Because they are saying, if we are not ready with the product, our competitor will take the market. It's also about preserving the market. So, a massive shift has happened.

So, I think we are much better, but I think a lot can be done. Especially with trucks, in medium and heavy-duty, we are still starting. That is one vehicle segment which has to mature over the course of the coming years. Car sales have increased, but have to do more. So, if you look at the 2030 target of 30%, I think we are still far behind, but this year, there has been a massive change.

**Do you think the government should announce mandates or targets for specific vehicle segments? Because every segment is having a different pace of adoption and has different challenges involved.**

I think mandates drive scale. Subsidies will drive initial adoption. But scale cannot come through subsidies, so it only needs regulation. Delhi EV Policy is one such regulation, but we also have a couple of other policies like the fuel-efficiency regulations — the CAFE targets — which we have still not finalised. Once we have these supply-side policies, like efficiency regulation and phase-out targets, electrification will pick up massively.

**The government is also experimenting with hydrogen powertrains for trucks and buses. Do you think hydrogen has any significance, and is it realistically feasible to commercialise them?**

In terms of technology, it is possible, but I don't think economics will work into it. The industry is also pretty clear, but they are avoiding this transition, and one way to avoid this transition is to also have an alternative narrative. So, technologically, hydrogen is a possibility; we are not saying no. But economics will not help. At least in India, I think it is fairly clear: no one is betting on hydrogen, at least in the heavy trucking space.

**As Dr Hanif Qureshi said during his keynote address, the Ministry of Heavy Industries is considering a new scheme to further push the adoption of electric buses, this time targeting private operators. Could the proposed scheme promote their use in applications such as school and staff transport, where adoption currently remains minimal? Do these segments have any specific requirements that the policy would need to address?**

Private operators should be an important part of any new electric-bus scheme, given that they account for around 92% of India's bus market. School buses are particularly relevant because these buses operate on predictable routes and schedules and can be well suited to electrification. A future scheme should therefore include these segments while addressing their specific needs, including affordable financing, adequate charging infrastructure, and operational requirements. This could help accelerate adoption in a segment where electrification has so far remained limited.

**Would you say the sharp increase in electric-car adoption in 2025 of around 80% is mainly the result of more models and greater competition, or are there broader structural changes in consumer demand? What would it take for electric cars to cross 10% of new-car sales?**

The sharp increase in electric-car adoption reflects both greater model availability and competition, as well as changing consumer preferences. To take EVs beyond 10% of new-car sales, however, stronger structural policy measures will be needed.

More stringent fuel-efficiency regulations (CAFÉ) and a phased approach to reducing new ICE vehicle registrations could provide a stronger market signal to manufacturers and consumers. The Delhi EV Policy, particularly its impact on two-wheelers, shows how targeted policy interventions can accelerate adoption.

**Should the government reintroduce subsidies for electric cars, specifically budget models priced between 500,000 and 1.5 million rupees?**

At the state level, we are seeing support. If you look at the Delhi EV Policy and Haryana EV Policy, registration tax is waived off on EVs priced up to 3 million rupees (approx. 27,300 euros).

**How do you see electric truck adoption scaling up? According to JMK Research & Analytics, registrations more than doubled in 2025, but the overall volume stood at just about 570 units. The Ministry of Heavy Industries aims to support 5,643 electric trucks under the PM E-Drive scheme, yet we appear to be a long way from reaching that figure. What do we need to do?**

The only thing that is hampering the uptake is the availability of CODs (Certificate of Deposits or scrapping certificates). For getting subsidy for electric trucks, you need to scrap old trucks of equivalent or higher weight. The problem is that 55-tonne trucks, which have maximum demand, only came in 2018. So, these trucks are not ready to be scrapped. That's the problem.

So, now the government is trying to work on some other regulations to avoid this. That's what we are saying, that please relax this requirement of scrapping equivalent or higher-weight trucks. That's the only issue.

**With the summit emphasising localised battery manufacturing, what policy support is needed to make batteries manufactured in India cost-competitive? Also, is India missing out on critical investment in this sector because of the geopolitical tensions with China?**

Localisation of battery manufacturing ultimately depends on scale, because that is when economies of scale can make Indian production cost-competitive. The key policy requirement is therefore to create demand certainty for OEMs and manufacturers through a predictable regulatory framework. This will give companies the confidence to invest in local battery cells, components and the wider supply chain.
