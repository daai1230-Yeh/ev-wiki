# EU and Indonesia agree free trade deal to cut tariffs on cars

**Author**: Cora Werwitzke | **Date**: 2026-11-23

---

The EU and Indonesia have signed a far-reaching free trade agreement and an investment protection agreement. Under the deal, Indonesian import tariffs on European cars, which currently reach up to 50 per cent, are to be phased out over the next five years. The EU also expects to gain better access to key raw materials.

After around ten years of negotiations, the EU and Indonesia have agreed on a free trade deal that is intended to gradually eliminate tariffs on almost all trade in goods between the two sides and facilitate new investment. According to the European Union, once the agreement is fully implemented, tariffs on up to 98.5 per cent of product categories will be abolished. The European Commission estimates that the deal could save EU exporters around €600 million in customs duties annually.

The agreement is also an important development for the automotive industry. European manufacturers currently pay import tariffs of up to 50 per cent on cars shipped to Indonesia. Under the agreement, these tariffs are to be phased out within five years. The Commission also expects new opportunities for EU investment in Indonesia, ‘notably in strategic sectors such as electric vehicles, electronics and pharmaceuticals‘.

The EU also expects the agreement to improve its access to raw materials. Indonesia is a leading producer of nickel and cobalt. The Commission says: “The agreement will provide EU manufacturers with stability and predictability on access to these materials, strengthen the EU’s strategic supply chains and protect them against disruption by sudden economic or geopolitical shocks.”

Over the next 15 years, the EU forecasts that free trade will increase EU exports to Indonesia by almost 40 per cent, ‘with particularly strong gains in machinery, motor vehicles, and other metals’. Indonesian exports of goods and services to the EU are also expected to rise by more than 14 per cent over the same period, according to the Commission’s estimates.

However, the agreement still requires several final steps before it can enter into force. These include approval by the European Parliament. Provisional application is also planned once the necessary internal procedures have been completed. The European Commission expects this to happen in early 2027.

With a population of around 290 million, Indonesia is considered the world’s third-largest democracy. The island nation has vast nickel reserves and is seeking to establish more of the material’s processing industry domestically. This includes battery factories as well as vehicle plants for electric cars. To secure supplies for its growing domestic industry, Indonesia even temporarily halted nickel ore exports.

Several Asian companies have subsequently announced major investments and specific e-mobility projects in Indonesia, some of which have already been completed. In the summer of 2024, for example, Hyundai and LG Energy Solution opened Indonesia’s first battery cell factory. Hyundai had already opened its first vehicle plant in Indonesia in 2022. More recently, Toyota and BYD have also started building electric cars locally. Battery giant CATL also broke ground on a battery cell factory in Indonesia in 2025, together with its joint venture partner Indonesia Battery Corporation. According to local media, production is expected to have started this month.
