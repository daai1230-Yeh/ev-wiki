# Interview with Hubject CEO Christian Hahn: "The real bottleneck is siloed thinking"

**Author**: Peter Schwierz | **Date**: 2026-08-31

---

How open is the charging ecosystem really – and what does it take for smart charging, Plug&Charge, and bidirectional charging to finally scale? Ahead of icnc26, Hubject CEO Christian Hahn discusses standards, charging silos, the V2G business case, and the unique charging requirements for electric trucks.

When the charging infrastructure industry gathers at the intercharge network conference (ICNC26) at Berlin-Tempelhof Airport in early September, the focus will be on far more than simply expanding the number of charging points. Charge point operators (CPOs), eMobility service providers, car manufacturers, energy suppliers, and hardware and software providers will discuss how to transform the growing infrastructure into an intelligent, interconnected, and economically viable system. Around 10,000 participants are expected at this year's event.

One individual who has witnessed this development since the early pioneering years of electromobility is Christian Hahn. He joined Hubject in 2012 and has served as CEO of the Berlin-based company since 2015.

**Mr Hahn, the market for conventional charging and roaming is now quite mature. What is still lacking in smart charging – and is technology posing a bottleneck or something else?**

Charging battery-electric passenger cars and the interoperability between charging networks have developed significantly. Today, you can travel across Europe with a single charging app and access the majority of public charging points. This has been achieved because the industry agreed on a common technical foundation.

What is still missing is this depth of collaboration at the next level of smart and bidirectional charging. Here, grid operators, energy traders, fleet operators, and charge point operators must coordinate in real time.

The technology is largely available, and regulation is also driving standardisation forward. In my view, the real bottleneck is the industry's willingness to overcome siloed thinking. Companies that could integrate sometimes choose not to because it is more advantageous in the short term to keep customers within a closed system. Now, we must transfer the insights gained from roaming to smart charging.

**Everyone talks about an open ecosystem. What does "open" mean in this context – and who must relinquish control or lock-in?**

No one has to give up the customer relationship. Interoperability in public charging already proves this. A charge point operator retains their charging points and commercial conditions but becomes accessible to more eMobility service providers, potentially increasing their utilisation.

What must be abandoned is lock-in, not ownership. What is needed are common technical standards and non-discriminatory access instead of proprietary exclusivity.

An open system must be built on non-proprietary standards such as ISO 15118 and OCPP. Qualified charge point operators, electric mobility service providers, or vehicle manufacturers must be able to connect under the same technical and commercial conditions. There should not be a lock-in when exiting, so a partner must be able to leave the platform, or work with multiple platforms without losing functionality.

**If open systems are meant to benefit the customer, why are charging prices and especially roaming surcharges still so opaque?**

The criticism is understandable and reflects a real gap between what the market has delivered so far and what drivers expect. Hubject operates at the B2B infrastructure level. Prices for drivers are set by the charge point operator and the eMobility service provider, not by Hubject. True transparency must therefore emerge across the entire value chain.

**How market-ready is ISO 15118-20?**

The AFIR provides a common reference point for the industry: new and modernised public charging points must support ISO 15118-2 from January 2026, and ISO 15118-20 for new and modernised public and private charging stations from January 2027. This creates a shared interoperability layer.

We are already seeing strong interest in the mTLS use case of ISO 15118-20, particularly among commercial charging companies and electric truck operators. The focus on security is also growing for home charging. However, smart and bidirectional charging will take longer to transition from pilot operations to full commercial deployment because more parts of the system must work together simultaneously.

Of course, there will be a mixed installed base during the transition. This is precisely why testing and certification are important. A charge point operator or vehicle manufacturer must be able to switch to a new standard version with confidence, without risking failed charging sessions.

**Plug&Charge is based on a complex certificate and PKI structure. Why hasn't this been adopted more widely yet?**

Adoption depends on two things coming together: infrastructure that supports Plug&Charge and vehicles that have the function enabled. Vehicle manufacturers often do not enable the feature for an entire model range at once but rather per model and market. As a result, the visible adoption on the road lags behind what is technically possible.

In Europe, there are now more than five million electric vehicles with Plug&Charge, representing a market share of around 40 per cent. It is also the preferred approach for charging trucks. The technology works. Now, the focus must be on further expanding its availability on both the vehicle and infrastructure sides.

## "Economic viability must be visible and tangible"

**Which V2G use case will first become a truly scalable business: private passenger cars, corporate fleets, or commercial vehicle depots?**

Corporate fleets and commercial depots are the more likely frontrunners. A single operator, predictable processes, and a controlled charging environment make the business case easier to demonstrate than coordinating millions of individual private drivers.

Private passenger cars represent the largest volume in the long term. However, electricity tariffs, driver behaviour, and manufacturer conditions must align here. This segment is therefore expected to scale later. If fleets and depots prove the model first, it will later facilitate the business case for private passenger cars.

**Who pays for the additional technology – and how much of the economic benefit must reach the driver or fleet operator?**

Costs and benefits are currently unevenly distributed along the chain. Vehicle manufacturers invest in hardware, charge point operators and energy providers in backend intelligence, and the driver bears additional complexity without always seeing a clear benefit.

The players who can monetise grid flexibility, such as grid operators and energy traders, have a strong incentive to finance the necessary intelligence. At the same time, a relevant portion of this value must reach those who provide their battery capacity.

Economic viability must be visible and tangible. The compensation must be worth the behavioural change. This is precisely what the industry must still prove on a large scale.

**Are too many players currently trying to secure a piece of a V2G pie that hasn't even been baked yet?**

Early positioning for future advantages is normal in a young market and not inherently unhealthy. It becomes problematic when this positioning is achieved through closed, proprietary systems. This fragments the market before it can even scale. That is why it is crucial to establish interoperability early before such silos become entrenched.

## Opportunistic charging not an option for electric trucks

**Do we need globally uniform standards at all – or is global interoperability sufficient?**

Full global standardisation is unlikely in the short term. Regions will continue to develop their own technical implementations and market structures. However, architectural principles can and should be shared globally: interoperability, open standards, and trustworthy authentication. The specific protocol stack can still differ from region to region.

**Does the commercial vehicle market need a new transaction logic?**

Roaming for passenger cars was developed for a more opportunistic and flexible use case. But commercial fleets need predictable, guaranteed access – a different operational logic based on reservations and reliability rather than opportunistic charging.

This doesn't require a complete restart. The existing roles – charge point operators, electric mobility service providers, and platform operators – need additional capabilities: reservation systems, access controls for secure locations, more comprehensive truck-specific site data, and authentication that is seamless enough to make Plug&Charge a necessity rather than just a convenience.

This is precisely why we have developed a cross-provider reservation platform for truck charging. It's embedded in a broader ecosystem with companies offering access or energy management solutions. For me, this is a practical example of how collaboration in the smart charging ecosystem can work in the future.

*ICNC26 takes place from 1 to 3 September 2026 at the former Tempelhof Airport in Berlin.*
