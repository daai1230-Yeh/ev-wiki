# A steep learning curve: how Spedition Stickel is building its battery-electric truck ecosystem

**Author**: Nicole de Jong | **Date**: Jul 9, 2026

---

The German logistics company Stickel is putting more of its freight on electric trucks, with a fleet of ten now in operation. Managing director Christian Stickel discusses investment willingness, initial complexity, and the confidence needed to embrace a new energy future.

A few years ago, when Christian Stickel's daughter asked him what he was doing for the environment, the company boss was at a loss for an answer. The next question followed quickly: why wasn't he operating a photovoltaic system on his company premises? "At the time, it didn't make economic sense," recalls the managing partner of Spedition Stickel, based in Nagold near Stuttgart. However, his daughter's persistence and, later, the energy crisis fundamentally changed his perspective on sustainability.

Today, Baden-Württemberg-based Spedition Stickel is one of the pioneers in the use of battery-electric trucks among Germany's medium-sized haulage firms, and is participating in a real-world laboratory for grid-supportive electric truck charging by NetzeBW. Stickel operates ten battery-electric vehicles daily, though procurement required significant lead time — initial discussions with energy provider EnBW took place as early as 2020, when the advice was still to wait, as the technology was considered too expensive for a medium-sized company with around 100 employees, a fleet of 69 trucks, and annual turnover of €15 million. "When the state of Baden-Württemberg later introduced funding programmes for charging infrastructure and vehicles, the situation changed."

## A successful funding bid

Stickel and his team began with detailed planning, analysing peak loads, developing a power supply concept, and applying for funding for ten battery-electric trucks. They received grants totalling around €240,000 for charging infrastructure, plus €75,000-€85,000 per vehicle toward the electric trucks. The company deliberately opted against a cautious start with a single vehicle: "We wanted to implement a large-scale solution to assess organisational processes, driving profiles, and economic viability more quickly than with a single test vehicle."

At the end of 2024, the first six electric Mercedes eActros 600 trucks entered service, followed by four MAN eTGX models. Behind a dedicated 1,000kW transformer, there are currently four fast-charging stations with eight charging points total: two Alpitronic chargers up to 400kW each (flexibly splittable power), and a Kempower system expandable by six more points. An intelligent load management system controls power flows across the trucks.

## New contracts secured

Customer requirements played a key role in the purchase decision. The haulage company works for automotive manufacturers and suppliers such as Mercedes-Benz, Porsche, and Bosch. "Tenders increasingly included requirements for low-emission transport," Stickel reports — for one client, the planned use of battery-electric trucks even became a prerequisite for collaboration.

Stickel now deploys vehicles in both regional and longer-distance routes, gradually expanding its MAN eTGX Ultra-Lowliners to routes such as Düsseldorf, Bremen, and Bayreuth, charging en route at fast-charging hubs up to 400kW, with legally mandated driver breaks often combined with charging times.

Battery-electric vehicles remain significantly more expensive than diesel trucks, but Stickel expects Chinese manufacturers entering the €180,000-€220,000 price segment to increase competitive pressure in Europe, potentially forcing German manufacturers to adjust prices toward €150,000 in the long term.

## Significantly higher mileage

Diesel trucks typically have an economic service life of 600,000-800,000 km. For battery-electric trucks, manufacturer specifications state batteries are designed for up to 1.2 million km or around 1,800 charging cycles — "these figures are not intended to be fully utilised in practice, but they indicate a significantly longer service life."

Driver acceptance was a key success factor; some persuasion was initially needed, but "once someone has driven an electric vehicle, they don't want to switch back to a combustion engine." Drivers appreciate the quieter, more relaxed driving experience and high torque for overtaking on hills. There is already high demand for the company's eleventh battery-electric truck, arriving shortly.

Depot charging remains key to cost-effective fleet operation. Stickel already relies on photovoltaics and is planning further expansion — a new 6,000 sq. metre logistics hall, opening end of February 2027, will include a ~650 kWp photovoltaic system supplemented by a 4-megawatt-hour battery storage system, aiming to reduce dependence on fluctuating diesel and electricity prices. Stickel plans to eventually open its charging hub (up to 1.6 megawatts) to third parties.

## Thorough preparation is essential

Electrification involves far more than purchasing new vehicles — the complexity is often underestimated. "In addition to the charging infrastructure, several IT systems are required to integrate load and energy management with billing." Integration of photovoltaics, battery storage, vehicle data, and charging planning remains true pioneering work. Collaboration with customers is crucial too, since technical issues or delays can occur with new technology — "clients must support this transformation process and be willing to pay more."

Stickel's key recommendation for other companies: thorough preparation, considering charging infrastructure, energy supply, and IT from the outset, plus early involvement of drivers, dispatch teams, and customers. "For us, there is no turning back. We are continuing down this path — consistently and decisively." He believes battery-electric trucks will be the central technology in road freight transport in the long term, even if challenges remain.
