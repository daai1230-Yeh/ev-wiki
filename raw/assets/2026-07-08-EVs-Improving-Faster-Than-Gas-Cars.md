# I've Seen More Improvement In Two Years Of Reviewing EVs Than I Did In A Decade Testing Gas Cars

**Author**: Mack Hogan | **Date**: 2026-07-07

---

When I left Road & Track to join InsideEVs, my car enthusiast friends were surprised. I was trading in the glamorous world of six-figure supercars and earth-shaking monster trucks for the practical business of charging infrastructure and battery chemistry.

In the two and a half years since, I can say with certainty that I've seen more exciting progress in the EV sector than I have in the last ten years of internal-combustion development.

**The Best Gas Car Has Already Been Built**

The first car I ever reviewed professionally, in 2017, was a Volvo S90 powered by a 2.0-liter turbocharged inline-four. Today, the S90's SUV sibling, the XC90, remains with better cabin tech and tuning, but the engine/transmission combo is much the same — only 1 mpg better, thanks to a mild 48-volt hybrid system rather than gains in gasoline engine design.

The best-selling vehicle in America back then, the Ford F-150, offered 2.7-liter and 3.5-liter turbocharged V-6s and a 5.0-liter V-8. A decade on, it has the same engines plus a hybrid option. Chevy trucks suffered transmission problems on both 8- and 10-speed models, and cylinder-deactivation technology dinged the reliability reputation of the 5.3-liter V-8. Toyota's clean-sheet turbocharged V-6 for the Tundra led to over 100,000 recalled trucks.

It all shows that trying to make gas engines a hair more efficient often introduces a lot more complexity, leading to terrible reliability tradeoffs. Consumers want to spend less on fuel without more complexity, more repair bills, or less power. Electrification is the one thing dramatically improving automotive powertrain technology.

**EV & Hybrid Tech Saves The Day**

The Toyota RAV4 unseated the F-150 as America's best-selling vehicle last year, thanks to its hybrid-only redesign. Going hybrid tends to improve longevity and majorly boost efficiency.

The pace of improvement in the pure EV space is even more dramatic. When the author started at InsideEVs in early 2024, the quickest-charging EVs on sale in America charged from 10-80% in 18 or 19 minutes; Mercedes just launched a car that can do it in 11. The Chevy Blazer EV started at $58,590 back then — now it starts around $45,000, and the Equinox EV with over 300 miles of range starts at $35,000. The Lucid Air Grand Touring's range record of 516 miles hasn't budged, but its starting price has dropped by over $10,000, and models from Chevy, Cadillac, BMW, Volvo, and Lucid now also exceed 400 miles of EPA range. Only 21 EV variants exceeded 300 miles of EPA range at the end of 2023; by the end of this year there will be around 60.

Fast-charging infrastructure keeps expanding, EV batteries are proving to degrade slowly and rarely fail, and GM's once-buggy Ultium software has gone nearly two years without a meaningful bug for the author's daily-driven Blazer EV. Perhaps most importantly, EV choice has exploded: Toyota/Subaru, GM, Rivian, BMW, Mercedes, and Volvo now all offer polished, software-defined EVs, with affordable options like Slate's $25,000 EV pickup and a sub-$30,000 Ford alternative on the way.

Battery breakthroughs — high-silicon anodes, lithium-manganese rich chemistry, solid-state technology — promise to keep driving down costs while improving range and longevity. There are no equivalent gasoline breakthroughs on the horizon; after 150-odd years of iteration, the technology is nearing the end of the line.
