# EU-China hybrid import quota unlikely to apply to range extenders

**Author**: Florian Treiss | **Date**: 2026-11-23

---

The annual import quota of 350,000 hybrid vehicles agreed upon between the EU and China in October apparently does not include electric vehicles with range extenders. Instead, these cars are likely to continue being classified as battery-electric vehicles and thus be subject to the special tariffs on EVs, but no import quota would apply.

According to reports, electric vehicles with range extenders (EREVs) will likely continue to be treated as fully electric models in trade between China and the EU. As a result, they do not fall under the annual limit of 350,000 hybrid vehicles agreed between Brussels and Beijing in October, which allows China to export hybrid vehicles to the EU with an annual growth rate of six per cent – including plug-in hybrids with external charging capability.

However, this also means that vehicles with range extenders could remain subject to the EU anti-subsidy tariffs on Chinese electric vehicles, which can reach up to 35.3 per cent depending on the manufacturer. This classification can be both an advantage and a disadvantage for Chinese manufacturers: while there is no volume restriction on imports for such models, the aforementioned anti-subsidy tariffs still apply. If these are passed on to customers, the vehicles become more expensive to purchase; otherwise, profit margins shrink.

## Different classification for quotas and special tariffs

The distinction stems from the differing customs classifications of drivetrain concepts. In a conventional hybrid or plug-in hybrid, the internal combustion engine can directly drive the wheels. In an EREV, however, only the electric motor provides propulsion. The internal combustion engine serves solely as a generator to recharge the battery while driving. Additionally, these vehicles can be charged externally.

The European Commission already made this distinction during its investigation into Chinese electric vehicles in 2024. In Implementing Regulation (EU) 2024/2754, it explicitly clarified that battery-electric vehicles with an internal combustion engine as a range extender also fall under the special tariffs. The reasoning: in these vehicles, the internal combustion engine exclusively charges the battery. Accordingly, they are classified under tariff code 8703 80 10 for battery-electric vehicles. The new import quota for hybrid vehicles, on the other hand, refers to different tariff codes – namely 8703 40 10, 8703 50 00, 8703 60 10, and 8703 70 00. As a result, EREVs currently do not fall under the EU’s so-called safeguard investigation (more on this below).

## Four Chinese range extenders in Germany

This distinction currently affects a handful of Chinese range-extender models available on the German market. These include the Leapmotor B10 Hybrid EV, with prices starting at €32,900. The compact SUV combines a 1.5-litre petrol engine as a generator with an electric drivetrain. Above it is the larger Leapmotor C10 Hybrid EV, available from €38,600 and offering a WLTP total range of up to 986 km.

Xpeng only added a range extender to its German lineup last week. The Xpeng L03 PowerX Reev starts at €38,600. Its 1.5-litre petrol engine serves exclusively as a generator, while a 178 kW electric motor drives the rear wheels. The electric range is up to 215 km, and the combined WLTP range is 1,017 km. Additionally, Geely’s Zeekr brand has announced its luxury SUV, the Zeekr 9X, as its first range-extender model for the German market.

## Manufacturers may adjust their strategies

The exemption from the hybrid quota could provide Chinese manufacturers with an additional incentive to expand their range-extender offerings in Europe. Given that this technology already plays a larger role in China, it could also gain significance in the European market.

According to a representative of the EU Commission, Brussels and Beijing have agreed to limit Chinese hybrid imports to 350,000 vehicles in the first year, with the quota increasing by six per cent annually thereafter. However, the corresponding legal act has not yet been published. Instead, the EU Commission initiated a formal safeguard procedure on 30 October – three weeks after the agreement with China. This procedure is not limited to China but covers imports of hybrid vehicles (including plug-in hybrids) from all third countries.

The notice initiating the safeguard procedure states that total imports of the affected vehicles increased from 957,965 units in 2023 to 1,423,691 units in the period from 1 July 2025 to 30 June 2026—a 49 per cent increase compared to 2023. The safeguard procedure could lead to import restrictions on hybrid vehicles from all third countries. This would, in principle, also affect models manufactured in the UK or the US. Whether and in what form the EU will actually introduce such measures remains open.
