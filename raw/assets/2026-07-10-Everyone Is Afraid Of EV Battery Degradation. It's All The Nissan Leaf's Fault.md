# Everyone Is Afraid Of EV Battery Degradation. It's All The Nissan Leaf's Fault

**Author**: Andrei Nedelea | **Date**: 2026-07-09

---

The Leaf made battery degradation look terrifying, but most modern EVs are not aging the same way.

The Nissan Leaf is one of the original modern purpose-built electric vehicles, so it's what many people still picture when they think of an EV. It was advanced, likable, and genuinely important, but it also had one engineering flaw that may be responsible for giving EV batteries a reputation they are still trying to shake: its battery pack was passively air-cooled, leaving it with very little control over cell temperature. Leafs tended to lose battery capacity more quickly than other EVs, especially those with actively liquid-cooled packs, and especially in hot climates.

The high cost of a replacement pack is the real reason battery degradation worries EV owners so much. In a 2026 AAA consumer survey, the high cost of battery repair or replacement was the most-cited reason people said they were unlikely to buy an EV, at 56%, ahead of purchase price, long-distance suitability, range anxiety, and charging concerns. But battery degradation isn't as scary as Leaf horror stories suggest — many older high-mileage EVs are still on their original battery, some more than a decade old, and actual battery failure is exceedingly rare on modern cars.

For over 15 years and two model generations, the Leaf only ever came with air-cooled batteries; the first Leaf with active thermal management is the new third-generation crossover model. A New Zealand study based on 1,382 battery-health readings from 283 Nissan Leafs found that 30-kWh Leafs showed an annual battery health decline of 9.9% at two years old — a strikingly high figure compared to any modern EV with active cooling. Even the earlier 24-kWh cars weren't nearly as bad, degrading 3.1% per year on average. Nissan later issued a software update for some 30-kWh models because the battery controller could incorrectly report capacity, but the early owner experience had already done its damage.

Early Leaf forums were full of drivers watching battery bars disappear, especially in hot climates. One independent test in Arizona in 2012 found a car that could only drive 59 miles before running out of battery, at no more than two years old. Installing a new pack in an early first-generation Leaf was expensive too — in January 2014, Nissan priced a replacement pack at $5,499 before tax and installation. Third-party companies popped up to offer upgraded battery packs with more power-dense cells, but the Leaf's battery issues left a stain on the entire EV market.

According to the National Renewable Energy Laboratory (NREL), high or low average battery temperature can accelerate lithium-related capacity loss in any EV; high temperatures in particular speed up unwanted chemical reactions inside the cell. Active battery thermal management systems, now fitted to every EV currently on sale in the U.S. and standard on most EVs worldwide, counter this. A Geotab study published in January found the 2015 Tesla Model S, which uses liquid cooling, has an average degradation rate of 2.3%, versus 4.2% for the 2015 Nissan Leaf with passive air cooling. A 2014 Tesla Model S with over 100,000 miles still retains 85% of its original battery capacity after 11 years — a feat virtually impossible for a first- or second-generation Leaf.

Glycol-based liquid cooling systems are now found in almost every EV on sale, often paired with heat pumps for maximum efficiency, moving heat between the battery, cabin, drivetrain, chiller, and radiator as needed. Thermal management continues to evolve — Shell recently unveiled a one-off EV that fully immerses battery cells in coolant rather than cooling them indirectly, for more effective heat extraction. The industry has largely moved on from the Leaf's rudimentary air-cooled approach, and modern batteries can go hundreds of thousands of miles without crippling degradation.
