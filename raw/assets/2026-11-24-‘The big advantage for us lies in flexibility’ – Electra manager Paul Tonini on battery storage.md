# ‘The big advantage for us lies in flexibility’ – Electra manager Paul Tonini on battery storage

**Author**: Florian Treiss | **Date**: 2026-11-23

---

Electra is increasingly relying on battery storage systems for its fast-charging parks. In Germany alone, 20 storage systems are already installed, and this number is expected to reach around 40 by the end of the year. In this interview, Germany CEO Paul Tonini explains the advantages of the technology, why grid operators sometimes complicate its deployment, and why battery storage alone cannot solve the challenges of expanding the charging network.

Battery storage systems are now a fixed part of the expansion plans for many fast-charging providers. This includes Electra, the French company that recently made headlines with its acquisition of Mercedes-Benz’s 47 fast-charging parks in Europe. With this move, Electra now operates more than 800 charging parks with around 5,000 charging points across ten countries.

To discuss how important battery storage systems already are today – and why they will become even more significant in the future – we spoke with Paul Tonini, General Manager of Electra Deutschland. He not only provides concrete figures on the current and future inventory of battery storage systems but also explains their role in the business model, their limitations, and why he would like to see more support from policymakers and grid operators. He cautions: “Battery storage should not be seen as a panacea.”

**How many sites with battery storage systems does Electra now operate?**

Across Europe, we have now implemented around 50 sites with battery storage systems. In Germany, there are more than 20. By the end of the year, we will have around 40 sites here. We use external storage systems in combination with conventional charging stations, such as those from Alpitronic. This differs from integrated solutions, where storage and charging stations form a single unit. For next year, we plan to add nearly 60 more storage systems in Germany.

**What is the motivation behind using battery storage? Is it primarily about compensating for insufficient grid connection capacity?**

It’s a mix of various factors. In areas where we initially receive sufficient capacity via low-voltage connections, we can, for example, already build a charging park and later switch to a medium-voltage connection once the grid expansion is complete. This also allows us to reduce utilisation risk.

However, there is a certain contradiction here: a well-utilised charging park requires a lot of grid capacity to provide enough energy within 24 hours. The better a site performs, the less we actually need the battery there. In such cases, we could potentially deploy it at another location.

**What other benefits do battery storage systems offer at charging parks?**

Another aspect is grid compatibility. Some grid operators explicitly welcome the use of storage systems. For us, they are primarily an additional solution component that makes us more flexible in site development.

This is particularly interesting in urban areas. In Munich, for example, a medium-voltage connection often requires both a transfer station and a transformer station. This takes up a lot of space, which is expensive and scarce in cities. With battery storage systems, we can design the spatial arrangement more flexibly and do not need to place all components immediately next to each other.

**Nevertheless, many grid operators seem sceptical about using battery storage systems. What are the issues?**

For one, some grid operators do not necessarily see storage as a way to increase charging capacity. They tend to view it more like a household storage system, whose capacity they could, for example, limit to 4 kW. We then have to explain that while storage and charging stations form a technical symbiosis, they are two separate units that can operate independently at the grid connection.

A second issue is the so-called short-circuit current contribution at the grid connection point. With storage systems, there are concerns that, in the event of a fault, the full installed inverter capacity from the storage could be fed into the grid, even if the grid connection is dimensioned much smaller. Such technical questions can be resolved, but this requires dialogue and experience. Many grid operators currently lack a uniform concept for dealing with these systems.

For example, we have a site in northern Germany where we negotiated with the grid operator for a year over a corresponding grid connection contract for grey-current storage. Just before signing, the procedure was stopped by the grid operator. Now, we have been promised a flexible grid connection contract in Q1 2027. This is naturally frustrating, as we see significant customer potential there and would like to fully realise our vision for the site.

**How powerful are the battery storage systems you use? Could it be the case that, under high utilisation, they eventually run out of charge and charging power drops?**

On average, we install storage systems with a capacity between 500 kilowatt-hours and one megawatt-hour. With one megawatt-hour, it would take about 30 consecutive charging sessions to fully discharge the storage. However, energy continues to come in parallel via the grid connection. Additionally, the storage can be recharged during breaks in charging.

At a site with 40 to 50 charging sessions per day, it could still become tight. Competitors are already achieving such utilisation levels at motorway locations. In urban environments, we are often still some way away from this, partly because there is a denser distribution of charging stations there.

This is why, especially at motorway locations, we need to carefully consider whether a battery storage system is the right solution. If a large number of customers want to charge one after another during travel times, a sufficiently dimensioned medium-voltage connection is important. The battery can then additionally absorb peak loads, such as on Saturdays or at the start of holidays.

**Can battery storage systems actually help bring charging parks online faster?**

This depends heavily on the respective grid operator. If they agree with the concept, it can be faster. But if I first have to painstakingly explain the technical relationships and present the entire concept, it can take longer than delivering a transformer. It’s not necessarily cheaper either.

The big advantage for us lies in the flexibility in using the grid connection and managing utilisation. It also allows us to open up sites that we might otherwise have to exclude from the outset without storage.

**In addition to providing charging capacity, battery storage systems can also be used for energy trading or peak shaving. What role does this play at Electra?**

This is very site-specific. In low-voltage networks, for example, we can benefit from atypical grid usage. We can also store electricity during cheap periods and use it again when prices are higher. Capping particularly expensive peak loads is another option.

We have employees who intensively study which operating concept makes sense for which site. This can change over time. If the utilisation of a charging park increases, the additional management of the storage naturally becomes less interesting. Then, the focus is more on providing energy as cost-effectively as possible for the charging processes.

**How do you think policymakers and grid operators can make the use of battery storage systems and the expansion of charging infrastructure easier?**

First of all, battery storage should not be seen as a panacea. Politically, this is sometimes portrayed as such, but ultimately, it always depends on the respective grid operator and whether – and under what conditions – they accept a storage system.

As operators, we are all interested in expanding our core business – charging. For this, we need access to the power grid. At the same time, we handle the available capacities responsibly. But for this, we also need the backing of policymakers and grid operators.

Another problem is flexible grid connections. In some grid areas, for example, we are offered connections where we can build a charging park with 800 kVA and six charging points, but the grid operator reserves the right to reduce the capacity to 400 kVA for 680 out of 720 hours per month – and within those 680 hours, even further to as low as 200 kVA for up to five days. So, I invest in the complete infrastructure for 800 kVA, but this capacity is not guaranteed.

Such a charging park can cost up to one million euros. For this, I need reliable planning to be able to recoup the invested money. This is difficult under such conditions.

Thank you for the interview, Mr Tonini!
