# Sumitomo Chemical aims to make solid-state batteries more affordable

**Author**: Jasmin Apitzsch-Krause | **Date**: 21.07.2026

---

Sumitomo Chemical is preparing for the series production of a new electrolyte for solid-state batteries. The halide-based material is intended to combine high performance with simpler and more cost-effective manufacturing.

Sumitomo Chemical has developed a solid-electrolyte battery in collaboration with Kyoto University and Tottori University. The technology is based on halides and uses chlorine, among other elements, positioned as an alternative to sulphide and oxide electrolytes. Sulphide electrolytes are considered promising for solid-state batteries due to their high ionic conductivity; Sumitomo Chemical claims to have optimised its material to achieve comparable ionic conductivity, though specific figures have not been provided.

The key advantage may lie in production. Sulphide electrolytes are sensitive to moisture and require tightly controlled manufacturing conditions. The new material is expected to be less demanding to process, and battery manufacturers could partially utilise existing lithium-ion battery production facilities. The goal is to achieve manufacturing costs on par with today's high-performance lithium-ion batteries.

This could be particularly relevant for electric vehicles, where solid-state batteries promise higher energy density, shorter charging times, and improved safety, but widespread adoption has so far been hindered by high costs and scaling challenges. The company is currently having the material evaluated by potential customers and plans to initially establish mass production in Japan. No timeline or capacities have been disclosed yet.

Several companies in Japan are working towards commercialising solid-state battery technology by 2027 or 2028: Toyota with Idemitsu Kosan on sulphide solid electrolytes and Sumitomo Metal Mining on cathode materials, and Nissan aiming to commercialise its own solid-state batteries by fiscal year 2028. Sumitomo Chemical's halide electrolyte represents a different technological approach compared to the widely favoured sulphide systems.
