# Is MCS the final word in megawatt truck charging?

**Author**: Peter Schwierz | **Date**: 2026-07-30

---

The Megawatt Charging System (MCS) is entering the market as the standard for high-power charging of electric trucks. But it is not the only concept under development. From six-megawatt connectors to automated underbody charging, we examine the technologies that could complement—or challenge—MCS in specific applications.

With the first MCS-compatible trucks and charging stations now entering commercial service, it may seem premature to ask whether the Megawatt Charging System (MCS) will remain the dominant charging interface for heavy-duty transport.

MCS has only just moved beyond the development stage and is now entering the market while the final steps of international standardisation are being completed. Truck manufacturers are launching MCS-compatible vehicles, charging equipment, cables and vehicle inlets, and the first truck charging hubs are already operating with installed hardware. With technical specifications converging globally, MCS appears well on its way to becoming the standard interface for ultra-fast charging of battery-electric heavy-duty trucks.

However, alternative concepts are already emerging. At Power2Drive Europe trade fair in Munich in June, several companies presented solutions designed either to exceed the capabilities of MCS or to eliminate the conventional charging connector altogether. Start-up akroVA, for example, demonstrated a manually operated charging plug rated for up to six megawatts. At the same event, the Fraunhofer Institute for Transportation and Infrastructure Systems (IVI) unveiled an automated underbody charging system for electric trucks that is designed to deliver up to 4.5 megawatts.

**MCS currently sets the standard**

For public high-power charging, MCS currently has no viable alternative. Development of the Megawatt Charging System began in 2018 under the umbrella of the industry association Charging Interface Initiative (CharIN). In early 2026, the central technical specification for the MCS connector, IEC TS 63379, was published. It defines operating voltages of up to 1,250 volts and currents of up to 3,000 amperes, allowing peak charging power of up to 3.75 megawatts.

MCS combines this charging performance with a key advantage: it is designed as a uniform, cross-manufacturer standard. Any MCS-compatible truck should in principle be able to charge at any MCS charging point, regardless of vehicle or infrastructure supplier, provided both comply with the specification.

**akroVA aims to deliver 6 MW through a single plug**

The most direct alternative to MCS is another charging connector. At Power2Drive Europe, akroVA, a German start-up spun out of paXos Solar, presented a system capable of delivering up to six megawatts. The technology uses multiple ring-shaped contact surfaces instead of elongated pins, allowing the connector to be inserted in any orientation. Test documentation shows the system transmitting 4,000 amperes at 1,500 volts (six megawatts) continuously for 46 minutes, with a scalable design supporting three to twelve megawatts.

**Fraunhofer IVI eliminates the need for manual connection**

The Underbody Charging System (UBCS) developed by Fraunhofer IVI takes a different approach: the truck drives over a ground-integrated charging unit, protective covers open, and a contact module connects to the vehicle's underbody, transferring energy conductively rather than wirelessly. It supports voltages up to 1,250 volts and charging capacities up to 4.5 megawatts, and complies with IEC 61851-26. The system is well suited to depots, ports and autonomous vehicle operations where a driver-operated plug is impractical. Standardisation of the interface is expected in the first half of 2027.

**Pantographs and multiple-plug approaches**

Pantograph systems, already established in electric buses, offer automated charging up to one megawatt via roof-mounted or station-mounted contacts, suited to fixed-route fleets. Chinese manufacturers like Sany instead distribute power across multiple cables (up to 800 kW via two or four connections), while ChaoJi/Ultra-ChaoJi supports up to 1.8 MW in the Chinese and Japanese markets.

**Broader alternatives**

Overhead catenary systems, inductive road charging and battery swapping avoid the need for very high peak charging power altogether but require far more extensive infrastructure changes, and have seen mixed results in European trials.

**The application determines the interface**

MCS is likely to dominate public, cross-manufacturer charging hubs, while depots, ports and autonomous logistics may favor automated underbody or pantograph systems. The future of heavy-duty charging is likely to involve several complementary interfaces rather than one universal solution.
