# Munich study calls for revisions to EU CO2 regulations for cars

**Author**: Jasmin Apitzsch-Krause | **Date**: 2026-09-01

---

A study from the Technical University of Munich (TUM) is calling for changes to EU CO2 regulations for passenger cars, arguing that EU fleet limits fail to account for emissions from production, energy provision, and recycling.

The CirculaTUM research consortium at TUM examined the climate impact of cars over their entire lifecycle, from production and operation to end-of-life treatment. Drawing on 19 previously published studies covering 47 modelled scenarios, researchers found that battery-electric vehicles produce 41% fewer CO2 emissions on average than comparable internal combustion engines over their full lifecycle, with EVs performing better in around 92% of cases.

Results varied significantly between scenarios, however — depending on vehicle and battery production, electricity mix, and recycling, emissions can be 89% lower or up to 21% higher compared to a combustion engine vehicle.

The researchers argue this variation highlights a weakness in the EU's current regulatory approach. CO2 fleet limits are based on direct emissions during vehicle operation, meaning battery-electric vehicles are assigned zero grams of CO2 per kilometre — contrasted with the average 41% lifecycle reduction TUM found. The critique isn't that electric cars lack a climate advantage (present in the vast majority of scenarios examined), but that regulatory assessment doesn't reflect a vehicle's full climate impact: emissions from vehicle and battery production or charging electricity aren't included in the CO2 fleet value, and neither are savings from near-CO2-free steel, more decarbonised battery production, or raw material recovery through recycling. This means, the researchers say, the industry lacks regulatory incentives for additional CO2 savings along the value chain.

TUM calls this a "substantial misdirection" in European climate policy and proposes a lifecycle-based standard recognising verifiable emissions reductions regardless of whether they occur during production, use, or recycling — suggesting steel as a starting point given its relatively robust existing data foundation.

The study also questions the achievability of Europe's climate targets. Citing one of the 19 studies, TUM estimates the passenger car sector could achieve a CO2 reduction of around 80 million tonnes by 2030 if the planned electromobility ramp-up proceeds as planned — but meeting the self-imposed target would require around 188 million tonnes, leaving a gap of over 100 million tonnes (a figure from one evaluated study, not an independent TUM forecast). Europe's existing vehicle fleet compounds the challenge: combustion engine vehicles are still expected to account for around 78% of the European passenger car fleet in 2030, since rapid EV sales growth only gradually affects the overall fleet given cars' long service life.

The study lands amid active political debate: at the end of 2025, as part of its Auto Package, the EU Commission proposed reducing the CO2 reduction target for new passenger cars from 100% to 90% (vs. 2021) starting in 2035, with remaining emissions offsettable through credits for green steel and climate-neutral fuels. TUM argues this is insufficient — the study states the Auto Package's proposed credits could cover at most 10% of a manufacturer's required CO2 reduction — and calls for an additional mandatory, verifiable lifecycle assessment.

The study doesn't question electromobility's climate advantage but how CO2 savings are regulated, arguing future assessments should consider savings in materials, production, energy supply, and recycling — not just direct vehicle emissions.
