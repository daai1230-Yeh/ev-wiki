# ICCT: Relaxing CO₂ standards for passenger cars threatens jobs

**Author**: Cora Werwitzke | **Date**: 2026-10-28

---

At the EU level, there are signs of a relaxation of CO₂ fleet limits for passenger cars. An ICCT study warns that this could cost the European automotive industry up to 185,000 jobs. The auto industry itself, however, sees jobs at risk primarily due to the strict 2035 target currently in place.

The International Council on Clean Transportation (ICCT) summarised the impact of current political decisions on CO₂ regulations on employment in its 58-page study "Powering jobs: employment under the EU's passenger car CO₂ standards". Its core message: maintaining current targets – effectively phasing out new ICE vehicles by 2035 – would secure current employment levels, while relaxing them would endanger jobs. Keeping the standards, combined with additional instruments such as EU production incentives, could even generate job growth.

## ICCT warns of net job losses

A weakening of the 2035 targets might temporarily halt job cuts in ICE production, but would slow employment growth in EV manufacturing, battery production, recycling and charging infrastructure – a "net loss in employment". Job gains in battery production, recycling and charging can, depending on the scenario, offset losses in ICEV production.

## Current legislation

For 2025–2027, OEMs must reach a fleet average of 93.6 g CO₂/km (15% below the 2021 baseline); the target tightens to a 55% reduction by 2030 and 100% by 2035. In its draft "Auto Package" at the end of 2025, the European Commission proposed relaxing this. The majority of MEPs want only a 95% CO₂ reduction by 2035, with OEMs allowed to fall a further 7.5 percentage points below if they use climate-friendly European steel. This still has to pass further committee stages.

## Three scenarios

Lead author Sandra Wappelhorst: "Weakening the CO2 standards is being presented as a way to protect jobs, but our analysis shows the opposite." The EU should complement its standards with measures to strengthen domestic EV supply chains; "clinging to an obsolete technology like the internal combustion engine won't help workers in the long run."

- Baseline ("Current Standards"): employment under the rules as they stand.
- "Policy Measures": additionally, measures to strengthen value creation in the EU (e.g. local value-added guidelines as in the "Industry Accelerator Act", national approaches, production incentives), modelled as a higher EU production share of BEVs and PHEVs.
- "Delayed Standards": a five-year delay, with BEV and PHEV shares rising moderately by 2030 and reaching 100% BEV in new car sales by 2040.

The study deliberately excludes other influences such as automation, digitalisation, geopolitics and upskilling needs.

## Findings

Differences between scenarios are small until 2030. By 2035, keeping current standards leaves employment about 2% below 2025, while additional industrial measures could raise net employment by around 10% – roughly 320,000 additional jobs. Weakening the standards could push employment up to 6% below the 2025 level, equal to a loss of up to 185,000 jobs.

Passenger car production accounts for the largest share of the differences between scenarios, with battery production/recycling and charging infrastructure growing in importance. Peter Mock, Director of ICCT Europe: maintaining the current CO₂ standards will help move European manufacturers to the forefront of the EV market.
