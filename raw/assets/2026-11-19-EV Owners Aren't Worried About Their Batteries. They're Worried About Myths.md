# EV Owners Aren't Worried About Their Batteries. They're Worried About Myths

**Author**: Andrei Nedelea | **Date**: 2026-11-18

---

- 74% of surveyed EV owners identified myths as a leading barrier to adoption, ahead of price and apartment charging.
- Battery longevity topped the list of misconceptions, yet 65% weren't worried about their own battery's lifespan.
- The survey reflects existing owners' views, rather than directly measuring why other buyers avoid EVs.

The battery always seems to be the big thing people cite as a key concern with electric cars. Will it need replacing? Will it catch fire? And what happens to the grid when everybody plugs in at the same time?

If you ask someone who already owns an EV, you'll likely get very different answers. The 2026 Global EV Driver Survey from the nonprofit Global EV Alliance found that 74% of respondents identified myths as a leading barrier to EV adoption in their country.

That put misconceptions ahead of purchase prices, cited by 49%, and charging access in apartment buildings, at 47%. Respondents could select up to three points.

The survey gathered responses from 29,547 EV drivers across 34 countries and ran from September through October. Participants signed up through EV associations, volunteers, and enthusiasts, with global results weighted according to countries' shares of the EV fleet.

These are existing owners explaining what they think holds other people back. The findings don't prove that misinformation matters more than affordability to first-time shoppers, but they show how owners perceive the gap between living with an EV and hearing about one.

Battery longevity was the most commonly reported misconception, identified by 72% of respondents. Next came claims that EVs catch fire more easily (58%) and that widespread adoption would collapse the electricity grid (45%). Yet 65% disagreed with the statement that they were concerned about their own battery's lifespan; only 18% agreed.

There's evidence supporting that confidence. Ford collected battery health data from 36,000 Mustang Mach-E EVs. The 2021-model-year examples retained an average of 92% of usable capacity after 5.2 years and 55,000 miles. More than 2,000 Mach-Es with over 100,000 miles still had 91% of their original capacity.

Fire fears need similar context. Studies don't support the idea that EVs are more prone to fires than gas cars; data generally points to lower fire rates than ICE vehicles, though EV battery fires are notoriously difficult to extinguish and can reignite.

On the grid, smart charging can help by replenishing cars when demand is lower. Adding more EVs will require planning and grid upgrades, but they don't all need to charge at the same time.

Another 40% identified the claim that EVs aren't better for the climate as a common misconception. Lifecycle comparisons show battery manufacturing adds emissions upfront, but EVs generally make up for that through lower emissions while driving, depending partly on the electricity mix.

EVs are still expensive, and charging is difficult for people without a home plug — owners acknowledged both. But buyers shouldn't assume they'll have to pay for a new battery a few years down the road.
