# 'SHERLOCK' project aims to standardise black mass for battery recycling

**Author**: Jasmin Apitzsch-Krause | **Date**: 2026-09-02

---

A new German research project is developing standardised quality classes for black mass, a key intermediate product in the recycling of lithium-ion batteries, with the aim of making battery recycling more efficient.

During the recycling of lithium-ion batteries, mechanical processing produces black mass — a fine-grained material containing valuable raw materials such as lithium, nickel, cobalt, and graphite, recoverable in later processing steps. But black mass is far from uniform: its composition depends on the battery's cell chemistry, age, usage history, and the specific recycling process used. These differences complicate further processing — impurities can reduce the efficiency of downstream recycling and compromise the quality of recovered recyclates.

The newly launched SHERLOCK research project aims to develop methods for precisely and reproducibly determining the composition and quality of different black mass materials. A central objective is developing standardised quality classes for black mass. The project will examine material from industrial recycling processes alongside specifically produced model materials, investigating how certain impurities affect direct and hydrometallurgical recycling methods. Results will feed into a criteria catalogue for assessing black mass and assigning it to an appropriate recycling pathway. Researchers will also explore methods for removing impurities and hybrid process chains combining direct and hydrometallurgical recycling.

A variety of spectroscopic and magnetic analysis methods will be used. Because ensuring comparable results across different labs and recycling companies matters for future industrial application, SHERLOCK will develop uniform guidelines for sample preparation, calibration, and measurement conditions — technical specifications that will later be submitted to DIN and ISO standardisation committees.

Stronger characterisation of raw materials and contaminants in black mass could let downstream recycling processes be tailored more closely to the material being processed, reducing material losses and improving the quality of recovered battery raw materials — though the project must still demonstrate whether this can measurably reduce recycling costs.

Economic viability remains one of the biggest challenges for battery recycling in Europe. A recent study published as part of the EU Safeloop project estimates current losses at around €1.90 per kg of battery pack, with hazardous goods transport, dismantling, and uncertainties in material recovery as key cost drivers. Better-characterised black mass and tailored recycling processes could address some of these losses.

Regulatory requirements are also increasing: the EU Battery Regulation will soon mandate higher recovery rates for raw materials like lithium, nickel, and cobalt, and from August 2031, minimum proportions of recycled materials in new vehicle batteries will apply — requiring both sufficient recycling capacity and reliably defined quality of recovered materials. A recent TU München study separately calls for greater regulatory recognition of savings achieved through battery recycling and a stronger circular economy for raw materials.

The SHERLOCK project involves the MEET Battery Research Centre at the University of Münster, the Physikalisch-Technische Bundesanstalt, the Bundesanstalt für Materialforschung und -prüfung, the Fraunhofer Institute for Silicate Research, and the Öko-Institut, along with Pure Devices and phase VISION. Recycling industry partners are also providing black mass from industrial processes for the investigations.
