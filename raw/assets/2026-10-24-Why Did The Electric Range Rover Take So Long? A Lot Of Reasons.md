# Why Did The Electric Range Rover Take So Long? A Lot Of Reasons

**Author**: Brett Berk | **Date**: 2026-10-23

---

A fully battery-powered Range Rover was first promised to consumers at least five years ago, when Jaguar Land Rover (JLR) revealed its "Reimagined Strategy," in which it vowed to electrify every product in its lineup. But while promises of an imminent release date have been frequent during the past half-decade, it only just made it into our hands. When I met up with the Land Rover development team in Scotland this week, I asked point blank what went wrong.

Turns out, plenty. The main issue, according to program chief Simon Fairbrother, derived from creating a propulsion system that would meet the desired attributes. "When we set out, we were going to have an induction motor at the front," he says. The benefit of such a system is that the forward unit can be disconnected in ordinary on-road driving when all-wheel-drive capabilities are not needed, improving efficiency and range.

But during testing, especially high-temperature testing, such a setup couldn't deliver the desired, consistent Range Rover off-road capability. "It was getting too hot, and we were having to back off the power," Fairbrother says. Fortunately, the brand had developed a more robust permanent magnet motor setup for the rear drive unit. So its solution was to simply replicate that system for the front axle, which meant going back to model, test and validate the setup again.

Compromise is not exactly in the Range Rover vocabulary. The new electric version had to maintain a relaxing "magic carpet" driving style on-road, and a refined spacious cabin with a command driving position, alongside supreme off-road capability. "We felt like we had to get it right and not just rush to the market with something that wasn't right for the brand," says Rachel Hogg, Range Rover product manager.

Fording depth in the new EV remains the same as in the gas-powered Range Rover, as are angles of ascent and descent. But the big rear motor means the Range Rover Electric can't accommodate a seven-seat variant. There is also a minimal reduction in departure angle due to a new aero-enhancing rear diffuser, and a small diminution of ground clearance and break-over angles, partially mitigated by the completely flat floor.

Land Rover has seen huge interest in its new EV from the North American market: "We've got over 80,000 genuine expressions of interest," Fairbrother says, "and a surprising majority are in the United States." Hogg says around 20% of the global waiting list already own an EV, and about 70% are new to the Land Rover brand. She believes fully electric vehicles will eventually constitute 10-20% of overall Range Rover sales, with the caveat that the brand "would need a crystal ball."

That uncertainty is why Land Rover remains steadfast in its powertrain-agnostic "Flex Architecture," which allows it to accommodate ICE, mild-hybrid, PHEV, and BEV propulsion within the same platform. "All of the vehicles are built down the same line in Solihull. So, we can flex to that demand... within weeks or months," Hogg says.

One intriguing detail: many high-net-worth potential clients are drawn to electrification "because they hate going to gas stations," Fairbrother says, since this cohort feels vulnerable in such public spaces. Charging at home provides reassurance, and Land Rover will offer a branded home charger solution.

The Range Rover Sport EV is launching in other markets and will likely come to the U.S. next year, and the five-door Range Rover GT will be released as an EV next year too, also on a flexible architecture. Asked whether these vehicles share componentry with the 2027 Jaguar Type 01, Fairbrother says, flatly, "Yes," but declines to elaborate; the propulsion system in this car is bespoke to Range Rover.
