# Waymo's Newest City Brings Its Biggest Challenge Yet: Rides In The Snow

**Author**: Mack Hogan | **Date**: 2026-09-01

---

Waymo opened up driverless rides in three more cities: San Diego, Tampa, and Denver. The last one is the most notable, since it's Waymo's first commercial deployment in a snowy area — so far, autonomous vehicles have mostly operated in the West and South, in cities with temperate climates.

Waymo expanded its robotaxi service to these three cities on Tuesday, marking a new phase in its aggressive expansion. Select riders can now hail fully autonomous Waymos without safety drivers in Denver, San Diego, and Tampa. The company's usual playbook is an invite-only period followed by a relatively quick ramp to fuller service — the Zeekr Ojai vans have already been seen testing in San Diego neighborhoods since last fall.

The bigger story is the new challenge Waymo is taking on: all its deployments so far have been in cities that rarely see snow. Nashville, Tennessee has been the coldest, getting under 5 inches of snowfall a year. Denver gets around 56 inches per year on average, per the National Weather Service. Waymo's announcement didn't explicitly say whether the robotaxis would operate in snowy weather — the company can and does pause operations during severe weather events (heavy storms in Miami, dust storms in Arizona). Per the company's safety blog: "If weather conditions deteriorate to the point that we believe it would affect the safe operation of our cars, Waymo vehicles are designed to come to a safe stop until conditions improve—just like a motorcyclist might stop under an overpass in heavy rain."

Still, there's reason to think Waymo is getting closer to true snow operation: it has done snowy testing in Novi, Michigan (outside Detroit) and Buffalo, New York, and previously announced service coming to Detroit and New York City (the NYC plan was blocked by local regulations) — not cities you'd target if you were afraid of snow.

San Diego and Denver are the first cities to exclusively receive the new Zeekr Ojai taxis, which run on Waymo's latest hardware and software stack.

The author notes that human drivers are generally poor in snow — impaired visibility and lower grip make it harder to react in time, and reactionary inputs often cause slides and collisions, with stability control systems typically the only thing preventing spin-outs. An autonomous vehicle that could apply instantaneous, consistent control logic to every input, while also using lidar for low-visibility conditions and HD maps to understand buried road features, could in principle be safer — though getting there will require significant further testing.
