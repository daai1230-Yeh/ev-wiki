# Everyone Is Afraid Of EV Battery Degradation. It's All The Nissan Leaf's Fault

**Author**: Andrei Nedelea | **Date**: Jul 9, 2026

---

The Leaf made battery degradation look terrifying, but most modern EVs are not aging the same way.

The Nissan Leaf is one of the original modern purpose-built electric vehicles, so it's what many people still picture when they think of an EV. The Leaf was advanced, likable, and forward-thinking in many ways, and genuinely important, but it also had one engineering flaw that may be responsible for giving EV batteries a reputation they are still trying to shake: its battery pack was passively air-cooled, an engineering compromise that left it with very little control over cell temperature. Nissan Leafs tended to lose battery capacity more quickly than other EVs, especially those with an actively liquid-cooled battery pack, and especially in hot climates.

Leaving the battery temperature up to the elements resulted in accelerated degradation and capacity loss in a vehicle that had less range than some modern plug-in hybrids to begin with. This gave many people the impression that EV batteries were as short-lived as those in their smartphones. But for the vast majority of EVs, that's not even close to true.

The real reason battery degradation worries EV owners so much is the very high cost of a replacement pack. In a 2026 AAA consumer survey, the high cost of battery repair or replacement was the most-cited reason people said they were unlikely to buy an EV, at 56%, ahead of higher purchase price, long-distance suitability, range anxiety, and charging concerns. But battery degradation isn't as scary as it may seem — many older high-mileage EVs are still on their original battery, some more than a decade old, and actual battery failure is exceedingly rare on modern cars.

For over 15 years and two model generations, the Nissan Leaf saw numerous improvements, but it only ever came with air-cooled batteries until the new third-generation crossover model, which is the first Leaf with active battery thermal management. A New Zealand study, based on 1,382 battery-health readings from 283 Nissan Leafs, found that 30-kWh Leafs showed an annual battery health decline of 9.9% at two years old — a very high figure compared to any modern EV with active battery cooling. Even the earlier 24-kWh cars weren't as bad, degrading around 3.1% per year on average. Nissan later issued a software update for some 30-kWh models because the battery controller could incorrectly report capacity, but the early owner experience had already done its damage.

Early Leaf forums were full of drivers watching battery bars disappear, especially in hot climates. One independent test in Arizona in 2012 (cited by Green Car Reports) found that one car tested had only 60-65% of its original battery capacity, able to drive just 59 miles before running out — the car couldn't have been more than two years old at that point. Installing a new battery pack in an early first-generation Leaf was expensive too: in January 2014, Nissan priced the replacement pack at $5,499 before tax and installation, assuming the dealer kept the old pack (valued at $1,000).

According to the National Renewable Energy Laboratory (NREL), high or low average battery temperature can accelerate lithium-related capacity loss in any EV. High temperatures in particular speed up unwanted chemical reactions inside the cell. Active battery thermal management systems aim to keep the pack within its preferred temperature window and reduce temperature differences between cells — they're fitted to every EV currently on sale in the U.S. A Geotab study published in January found the 2015 Tesla Model S, which uses liquid cooling, has an average degradation rate of 2.3%, versus 4.2% for the 2015 Nissan Leaf with passive air cooling. A 2014 Tesla Model S with over 100,000 miles still has 85% of its original battery capacity at over 11 years old — a feat that would be virtually impossible for any first- or second-generation Leaf.

Glycol-based liquid cooling systems are found in almost every EV on sale today. Most now team battery cooling with heat pumps for maximum efficiency, moving heat between the battery, cabin, drivetrain, chiller, and radiator as needed. Thermal management continues to evolve — Shell recently unveiled a one-off EV that fully immerses battery cells in coolant rather than cooling them indirectly, aiming for more effective heat extraction.

The world has moved on from the Leaf's rudimentary air-cooled battery management approach. Modern techniques are helping batteries go for hundreds of thousands of miles without crippling degradation. So while the Leaf was an early pioneer in many ways, the industry learned its biggest lesson and adopted active battery cooling systems for just about every model.
