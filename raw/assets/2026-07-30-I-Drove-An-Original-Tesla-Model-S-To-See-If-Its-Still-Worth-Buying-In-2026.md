# I Drove An Original Tesla Model S To See If It's Still Worth Buying In 2026

**Author**: Andrei Nedelea | **Date**: Jul 30, 2026

---

After 160,000 miles, this 2015 P85D still has its original battery, more than 186 miles of real-world range, and insane performance.

The most surprising realization after spending some time with a 2015 Tesla Model S P85D is that it doesn't feel like an old EV. It feels like an old luxury car, in a good way. That wasn't something I was expecting to say about it in 2026, 11 years after it first rolled off the line.

This example is the top-of-the-range Performance model, with a dual-motor powertrain that produces a combined output of 691 horsepower and 687 lb-ft (931 Nm) of torque. That is a lot of motivation, and it makes surprisingly light work of the car's 4,936 lbs (2,239 kg) mass, ripping from a standstill to 60 mph in 3.2 seconds.

That's the official claim, at least. But it's independently been measured to be even quicker than that. To experience all the acceleration the P85D has to offer, you have to put it in Insane+ mode. A message will pop up indicating that the vehicle has begun bringing its battery to the temperature its cells require to deliver all their available volts.

So the performance feels thoroughly modern, but the rest of the car doesn't look compromised by modern standards, either. Its design has its roots nearly two decades ago, when Tesla began developing the Model S in 2007. It showed the first design concept two years later, and the final production version, which began production in 2012, stayed remarkably true to the look of the 2009 study.

Today, the Model S doesn't look bad at all. Its shape has always had a whiff of Maserati or Jaguar, and as the Model S has matured, it is now starting to look like an older luxury car. It has the posh coupe-like sedan look that—back when it debuted—was nowhere near as common among mainstream cars as it is today.

The front fascia is what betrays its age. The facelift it got after April 2016 made it look a lot sharper and more modern by dropping the "grille," but today those newer cars just don't have the same old-world charm as an early Model S driving by.

Period reviews of the Model S's interior were generally positive. More than a decade on, the cabin appears to have aged gracefully. The Model S's Mercedes-sourced switchgear makes certain touchpoints feel like those of German cars from the era, and it felt pretty solid with hardly any noises making their way into the cabin while on the move.

With standard adaptive air suspension and smaller wheels, ride comfort was excellent, though the tester's air suspension had a leak causing it to sag after being parked for over two days. Steering feels somewhat vague and detached.

What hasn't aged well is the 17-inch portrait-style display. The graphics look rough and pixelated by today's standards, with occasional stuttering and lag, though it's still adequate to use today. This car's original MCU1 hardware can cause the infotainment system to lag; a later MCU2 upgrade is available from Tesla for $2,700.

An estimate via the Tessie app showed the car still had around 86% of its original battery capacity, delivering around 200 miles of real-world range. The car has the coveted free lifetime Supercharging option, transferable to new owners, saving the current owner thousands over two years of ownership.

If you're in the market for a cheap used EV, it's hard to argue with. It's the most historically significant car of its era, the blueprint that would define all modern EVs, and a decade later it remains a reliable, affordable used EV option whose charm still hasn't faded.
