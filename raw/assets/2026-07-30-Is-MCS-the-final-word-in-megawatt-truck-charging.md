# Is MCS the final word in megawatt truck charging?

**Author**: Peter Schwierz | **Date**: 30.07.2026

---

The Megawatt Charging System (MCS) is entering the market as the standard for high-power charging of electric trucks. But it is not the only concept under development. From six-megawatt connectors to automated underbody charging, electrive examines the technologies that could complement—or challenge—MCS in specific applications.

With the first MCS-compatible trucks and charging stations now entering commercial service, it may seem premature to ask whether MCS will remain the dominant charging interface for heavy-duty transport. MCS has only just moved beyond the development stage and is now entering the market while the final steps of international standardisation are being completed. With technical specifications converging globally, MCS appears well on its way to becoming the standard interface for ultra-fast charging of battery-electric heavy-duty trucks.

However, alternative concepts are already emerging. At Power2Drive Europe trade fair in Munich in June, start-up akroVA demonstrated a manually operated charging plug rated for up to six megawatts, while the Fraunhofer Institute for Transportation and Infrastructure Systems (IVI) unveiled an automated underbody charging system designed to deliver up to 4.5 megawatts. The debate is shifting towards whether a single interface such as MCS can meet the full range of charging requirements in heavy-duty transport — from public motorway charging hubs and depot operations to autonomous vehicles operating in ports and logistics terminals.

**MCS currently sets the standard.** Development began in 2018 under the Charging Interface Initiative (CharIN). In early 2026, the central technical specification, IEC TS 63379, was published, defining operating voltages of up to 1,250 volts and currents of up to 3,000 amperes, for peak charging power of up to 3.75 megawatts. MCS is designed as a uniform, cross-manufacturer standard and builds on the CCS communication and safety architecture. The connector is still connected manually, well suited to long-haul transport where drivers connect during a legally required rest break.

**akroVA aims to deliver 6 MW through a single plug.** The German start-up, spun out of paXos Solar, presented a system based on paXos' Cool-Load Megawatt Plug using ring-shaped contact surfaces instead of pins, allowing insertion in any orientation. Test documentation showed power transmission of 4,000 amperes at 1,500 volts (six megawatts) sustained for 46 minutes. The scalable concept is designed to support 3–12 MW. The connector has a smaller diameter than a CCS2 Combo plug (72mm) while supporting up to 6 MW with liquid cooling or 1.5 MW air-cooled. The bigger challenge is persuading manufacturers and infrastructure operators that a second physical interface is needed alongside MCS.

**Fraunhofer IVI eliminates manual connection.** Its Underbody Charging System (UBCS) has trucks drive over a ground-integrated charging unit; protective covers open and a contact module connects to the vehicle's underbody, conductively (not wirelessly). It supports up to 1,250 volts and 4.5 MW, complying with IEC 61851-26. It's well suited to depots, loading bays, and autonomous vehicles like driverless terminal tractors. The system is undergoing field trials, including a municipal refuse fleet in Germany; a first version of the interface standard is expected in H1 2027, with possible market introduction as early as next year.

**Other alternatives**: Pantograph systems (established in electric buses, Schunk offers systems up to 1 MW) suit fixed routes but need roof hardware and precise parking. Chinese manufacturers like Sany distribute power across multiple cables (up to 800 kW via 2-4 connections). ChaoJi/CHAdeMO 3.0 supports up to 900 kW, with successor Ultra-ChaoJi designed for 1.8 MW, though these remain focused on Chinese and Japanese markets. More fundamental alternatives — overhead catenary lines, inductive road charging, and battery swapping — reduce the need for high peak charging power but require far more extensive infrastructure changes; German overhead-line projects have lost political momentum after field trials.

**The application determines the interface.** MCS is likely to dominate publicly accessible infrastructure serving vehicles from multiple manufacturers — motorway hubs and truck stops — where interoperability outweighs alternative benefits. In depots and closed logistics operations, automated underbody charging or pantographs could be justified through more efficient operations, especially as autonomous vehicles become more common in ports, mines, and logistics terminals. Rather than replacing MCS, these technologies are likely to occupy distinct roles, with the future of heavy-duty charging involving several complementary interfaces rather than one universal solution.
