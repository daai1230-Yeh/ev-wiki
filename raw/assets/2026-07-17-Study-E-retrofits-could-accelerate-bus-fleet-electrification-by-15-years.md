# Study: E-retrofits could accelerate bus fleet electrification by 15 years

**Author**: Jasmin Apitzsch-Krause | **Date**: 2026-07-16

---

A study by Empa (the Swiss Federal Laboratories for Materials Science and Technology) highlights the potential of converting diesel buses to battery-electric drivetrains. Rather than waiting for vehicles to be replaced at end of life, e-retrofitting could accelerate the electrification of Europe's bus fleet by around 15 years — potentially crucial for meeting climate targets.

The bottleneck to electrifying Europe's bus fleets is not the availability of battery-electric buses but the long service life of existing vehicles, which remain in operation for an average of around 20 years. Even if only zero-emission buses were newly registered from 2035, Europe's fleet would not reach 95% electrification until 2057. E-retrofitting — replacing only the engine, gearbox, fuel tank and exhaust while keeping body, chassis and interior — could bring full electrification forward by around 15 years, offering faster decarbonisation, lower investment costs, reduced raw-material demand and greater flexibility.

Empa's life cycle assessment shows e-retrofitting generates around half the greenhouse gas emissions of producing a new battery-electric bus — roughly 58 tonnes of CO₂ equivalents saved per vehicle. Applied across Europe's fleet, the technology could deliver around 300 million tonnes of CO₂ savings by 2100, roughly equivalent to Spain's annual emissions. A retrofit also costs roughly half as much as a new electric bus, and the authors estimate it could lower total bus transport costs by 2.2%–3.2% by 2100.

The approach could also support Europe's electromobility industry by giving manufacturers time to expand capacity, and create opportunities for standardised retrofit kits and workshops. The study calculates that just 1% of today's automotive mechatronics workforce could retrofit up to 75,000 buses per year; at 5%, Europe's diesel bus fleet could theoretically be electrified in under three years.

Caveats: the economic analysis is based on model assumptions and pilot projects, with actual costs varying by platform, battery concept and range. Questions around certification, warranties, standardisation and charging infrastructure remain unresolved, and battery supply is likely to remain the main bottleneck. The study frames two questions for the bus market — whether decarbonisation requires replacing entire vehicles or only the drivetrain — and concludes that momentum will depend less on technical feasibility than on political and economic framework conditions, since funding programmes have so far focused on purchasing new electric buses.
