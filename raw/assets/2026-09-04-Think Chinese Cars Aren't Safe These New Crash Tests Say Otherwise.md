# Think Chinese Cars Aren't Safe? These New Crash Tests Say Otherwise

**Author**: Kevin Williams | **Date**: 2026-09-02

---

Westerners have historically been skeptical of the quality and safety of Chinese cars, and the concern wasn't entirely unwarranted — 2000s-era compact sedans like the Chery Amulet or Geely CK failed miserably at crash tests, leaving a lasting black eye for Chinese brands selling in Europe. But recent crash tests show things are very different now.

Euro NCAP, the region's main vehicle-testing organization, released test results this week for four new small electric cars, including three Chinese ones. The Geely EX2, Leapmotor B05, and Aion UT all earned five-star ratings. The Cupra Raval — the VW Group brand's take on the new Volkswagen ID. Polo — got four.

The star rating can be adjusted based on features beyond crash structure and deformation performance, including accident avoidance. The Raval lacks some standard crash-avoidance features that come on the other cars, like forward traffic alert or adaptive cruise control; Euro NCAP said an optional safety package raises its score to five stars. The EX2, B05, and UT all have those functions as standard. The Aion UT and Leapmotor B05's crash-avoidance scores of 74% and 77% respectively beat the Raval's 72%; the Geely E2 matches the Cupra's score with the option package.

It's hard not to be impressed by the quality, design, or value of Chinese cars at their price points — the Geely E2 starts just under €20,000 (about $23,000), one of the cheapest new EVs on sale in Europe. Lower prices have led some to wonder if these brands cut corners on safety or build quality, but the data suggests otherwise.

That doesn't mean Chinese cars are perfect. Euro NCAP criticized both the B05 and UT for being too touchscreen-heavy for vehicle functions, reflected in their "safe driving" scores — a measure of driver/occupant monitoring software and ease of use of functions while driving — of just 66%. The Geely, with a few more physical buttons for key functions, scored 79% in that category.

In China, the government has made safety and quality a bigger priority after years of rapid-fire car releases from dozens of brands, with agencies scrutinizing problematic designs (such as electronic door handles subject to a mass recall) more intensely and mandating factory audits to ensure quickly developed cars aren't cutting corners on safety.
