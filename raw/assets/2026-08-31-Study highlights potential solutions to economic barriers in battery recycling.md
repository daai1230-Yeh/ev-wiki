# Study highlights potential solutions to economic barriers in battery recycling

**Author**: Jasmin Apitzsch-Krause | **Date**: 2026-08-28

---

Recycling EV batteries is currently not economically viable in Europe even though it is technically well advanced. A study conducted as part of the EU project called Safeloop estimates the current loss at around 1.90 euros per kilogram of battery pack. Researchers estimate this could be rectified with different approaches – particularly in business models and more efficient processes.

An interim report by the HHL Leipzig Graduate School of Management, conducted as part of the European research project Safeloop, has identified several cost drivers that currently make battery recycling uneconomical in Europe. These include transport of hazardous goods, disassembly of battery packs, and uncertainties in material recovery. Transport costs are particularly high, as removed lithium-ion batteries are classified as hazardous goods and, according to the study, incur transport costs that are on average 16 times higher than those for standard freight.

It comes as no surprise then that companies specialising exclusively in battery recycling underperform economically compared to those with a broader business model. The economic viability of recycling is also critical in light of the EU Battery Regulation (Batt-EU-AnpG) introduced in 2025. This regulation stipulates that, as of August 2031, specific minimum proportions of raw materials used in new vehicle, industrial, and starter batteries must be sourced from recycling. The researchers at Safeloop have modelled the potential impact of current recycling losses on battery costs. They found, assuming a 20% recycling quota and full pass-through of losses, a battery pack could become around 6% more expensive.

## Recycling: second life does not solve the distribution problem

The study sees potential in the further use of decommissioned vehicle batteries. For example, if the battery of an electric bus is repurposed as a stationary storage system after its first life, its overall economic value across both usage phases could increase by 64 per cent, according to the study. However, this added value primarily benefits vehicle and storage operators — not necessarily the stakeholders who bear the higher costs of recycling and circular economy practices. The researchers identify this as a distribution problem that could hinder investments in the circular economy.

In addition, there are economic and technical requirements for second-life storage systems. Their deployment requires reliable information (such as that provided by the upcoming Battery Pass), competitive costs, and long-term guarantees. Eight expert interviews conducted as part of the study also reveal that safety concerns and investors' risk assessments are slowing adoption. The study shows that recycling costs could be significantly reduced with safer battery designs, shorter transport routes, more automated disassembly, specialised recycling structures, and standardised transport protocols, which could collectively cut costs by around 34 per cent. This would transform the current calculated loss of €1.90 per kilogramme of battery pack into a small profit of 13 cents.

The need for further development in scaling such approaches is also demonstrated by a cooperation launched in May between the EU and India. Both sides are jointly providing €15.2 million to advance recycling technologies for electric vehicle batteries. The focus includes more efficient material recovery, digitalised collection and sorting systems, and second-life applications. In India, a pilot plant is also planned to validate new processes under real-world conditions and advance them towards industrial application.

## New stakeholders to redistribute costs and revenues

For Safeloop, a key lever lies not only in recycling technology but also in the organisation of the value chain. The researchers propose new coordination stakeholders to manage battery packs and their materials across multiple life phases. This could enable a fairer distribution of costs and revenues among manufacturers, vehicle operators, second-life providers, and recyclers.

"Such stakeholders would own the batteries and the materials they contain. They could create market pressure for innovations in upstream processes while fairly redistributing the value derived from the second life of batteries," explains study leader Dima Smirnov from HHL. His conclusion: "Europe therefore needs not only better battery processes but also market-based business models that make circularity economically viable."

The Safeloop project was launched in 2024 and is scheduled to run for three years. It is funded by the European Union and involves 15 institutions from eleven countries, with a budget of approximately €4.7 million. The goal is to develop safer and more sustainable lithium-ion batteries for electric vehicles throughout their entire life cycle.
