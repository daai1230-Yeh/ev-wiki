# The All-Electric Jeep Recon Beats The Wrangler In One Key Way. But It's Still Flawed.

**Author**: Mack Hogan | **Date**: 2026-09-09

*The Recon is far more happy on the road than any Wrangler. But it's still a Jeep EV, and that's a big caveat.*

---

This is the first real electric Jeep. Sure, the Wagoneer S arrived in 2024 to a critical shrug, but that was a high-performance, on-road luxury vehicle—more Range Rover than Wrangler. The Recon Moab, though, rides on gnarly 33-inch tires, gets a locking differential, and offers an open-roof, no-doors experience. It's pure Jeep. For better, and for worse.

**Design & Overview**

You can spot it 50 yards out. While the Wagoneer S could be mistaken for just about any modern electric crossover, the Recon's broad stance, squared-off design, and prominent rear-mounted spare tire betray its origins. Currently available only in Moab guise—the EV equivalent of the hardcore Rubicon trim—the Recon looks fit for purpose.

The Recon rides on the same STLA Large platform as the Wagoneer S and the Dodge Charger, but with independent suspension at all four corners. Sharing roots with a muscle car suggests this isn't quite the pure-bred Wrangler alternative that some may assume, but that's ok.

The Wrangler has solid front and rear axles, which are great for off-road wheeling but terrible for on-road ride and handling, and brutal for steering. The Recon is more of a multi-tool: with an electro-mechanical locking rear differential, a variety of terrain modes, 9.1 inches of clearance, and strong approach/departure/breakover angles (33.8°/33.1°/23.3°), it's up for any forest road or field. But at a dedicated off-road park, it would be outmatched by Wranglers, Broncos and trucks with solid axles and more flexible bodies.

The payoff: the Recon's 670 horsepower and 620 lb-ft of torque deliver a 0-60 sprint in just 3.6 seconds. Combined with rack-and-pinion steering, a less creaky body, and less wallowy suspension, the Recon is a lot more capable on tarmac.

The Recon Moab offers 222 miles of range from its 100.5 kWh battery. At the launch event, journalists debated whether that's enough for a $66,995 vehicle. The reviewer wasn't surprised: a boxy, upright vehicle on knobby all-terrain tires needs a lot of energy to maintain 70 mph. Expect less than 200 miles between stops at highway speed. Charging is quick, though: Jeep says 10-80% in just 23 minutes. Longer-range, more street-oriented trims are coming. The starting price is about $17,000 more than a Wrangler Rubicon.

**Driving, Performance & Off-Road**

Unlike the Wrangler's older steering box, the Recon gets a road-oriented independent front suspension and modern rack-and-pinion steering — transformative for a knobby-tired Jeep on fast, straight roads. Still, on chunky all-terrain tires with more torque than a McLaren F1 and weighing 6,112 pounds, the Recon slid out at less than 30% throttle in light rain on a highway onramp.

New Jeep CEO Branden Coté called the Recon part "race car" — marketing speak, per the reviewer, who compares it more to a classic muscle car: fast off the line but sloppy in the corners, never fully settling down when driven hard. It handles pavement better than any Wrangler, though, and is quiet with no substantial rattles, making it comfortable for highway cruising.

Slow down and the shine wears off: low-speed throttle and regen calibration are janky, similar to the Wagoneer S and Charger Daytona. Precise throttle control below 5 mph is tricky, feeling binary at its worst. Regen and "creep" settings are confusing — turning creep off (not high regen) enables one-pedal mode, and it grabs the brakes too hard at low speed. The reviewer recommends regen set to low and creep on. Over a rock garden test course, the Recon bucked heavily even with off-road cruise control engaged; much of the planned off-road course was cut short due to rain, though the Recon did ford two feet of standing water. With a mechanical rear locker and 15:1 final drive ratio, the reviewer expects the Recon to out-climb the Rivian R2, though the R2 is more precise and better on-road.

**Interior & Tech**

The interior is utilitarian yet upscale, with a solid grab handle, bulky drive selector, locker/off-road mode switch, and either a standard dual-pane moonroof or a sliding retractable soft top. Doors, rear quarter windows and the rear windshield can each be removed in under a minute with no tools; door-mounted mirrors can be replaced with small mirrors at the mounting points, and a lockable center console secures valuables when doors are off.

The 14.5-inch central touchscreen runs Uconnect 5, flanked by a 12.3-inch driver display — both quick and well-designed. But EV-specific features are underbaked: the efficiency display is indecipherable (plotting miles/kWh and "charge" on a -2 to 2 scale against time by week), on-board navigation doesn't automatically add chargers, and Level 2 chargers get treated as viable road-trip stops. Climate controls live in the screen but are persistent software buttons, which the reviewer found easy to use despite missing a back button.

**Early Verdict**

The Jeep Recon isn't the best EV on sale, and it's an early effort from a brand not yet all-in on electrification (a gas variant is reportedly planned). But despite middling range, questionable timing and a high price, the reviewer liked it — it feels authentic, fun and unmistakably Jeep, solving his long-standing dislike of the Wrangler's road manners while keeping the doors-off, lockers-on appeal. At $67,000, though, he wants a more polished, fully-realized product — the Recon gets close, but isn't all the way there.
