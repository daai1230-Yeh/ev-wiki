# EU and UK could delay mutual EV tariffs once more

**Author**: Florian Treiss | **Date**: 2026-11-12

---

The EU Commission is reportedly considering postponing tariffs on the electric car trade with the UK, which were planned for 2027. London is also said to support a delay. The automotive industry warns of competitive disadvantages due to the looming ten per cent duty.

The EU and the UK appear to be moving towards another delay in the stricter rules of origin for electric vehicles. According to the Financial Times, the European Commission is currently consulting the industry on corresponding proposals, and the UK government is reportedly supporting a postponement. Without an agreement, a 10 per cent tariff would apply from 1 January 2027 on certain EVs traded between the EU and the UK. The UK produces vehicles such as the Nissan Leaf and several Stellantis electric vans for export to the EU, while a wide range of EVs are imported into the UK from the EU.

The background is the rules of origin agreed in the Brexit trade deal. The current transitional rules expire at the end of 2026. From 2027, at least 55 per cent of the vehicle's value must originate from the EU or the UK; for battery cells and modules 65 per cent, and for battery packs 70 per cent. Otherwise the 10 per cent tariff applies.

## ACEA warns of billion-euro burden

ACEA estimates around 520,000 electric cars and vans will be exported from the EU to the UK in 2027, worth €17.9 billion. Approximately 82 per cent – around 426,000 vehicles – would not meet the stricter rules, resulting in additional tariff costs of around €1.47 billion for 2027 alone.

In September, ACEA proposed a staggered delay: current requirements (battery pack assembly in the EU/UK sufficient) until end-2029; battery cells from the region from 2030; active cathode materials from 2032. The Commission is reportedly considering shorter transition periods.

## Commission examining options

At least two options are under discussion: a simple one-year delay, or a two-year scheme allowing manufacturers to offset non-compliant vehicles by selling compliant models in their home markets. No decision has yet been made. The European chemical industry opposes a further delay, having invested in European cathode active material production.

## Close interdependence of both markets

SMMT points to investments of around €72 billion in UK battery and EV production since 2017, though the UK still imports around two-thirds of its lithium-ion batteries. SMMT CEO Mike Hawes: "The UK and EU are bound by €80 billion in annual automotive trade and millions of jobs."

The EU and UK already delayed the stricter rules once, at the end of 2023. Serious negotiations on another postponement are set to begin after the EU-UK summit on 20 November.

## UK also wants to be part of 'Made in Europe'

The discussion takes place against the backdrop of the 'Made in Europe' policy under the EU's Industrial Accelerator Act. A package combining the tariff delay and equal treatment of the UK is conceivable. The prime ministers of Baden-Württemberg, Bavaria and Lower Saxony have called for including the UK, EEA and EFTA states in the 'Made in Europe' rules.
