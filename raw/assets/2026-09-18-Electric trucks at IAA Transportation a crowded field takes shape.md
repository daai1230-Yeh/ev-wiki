# Electric trucks at IAA Transportation: a crowded field takes shape

**Author**: Cora Werwitzke | **Date**: 2026-09-17 13:00

---

If IAA Transportation is a reflection of the global economy, it's clear electric trucks have firmly secured their place in industry. Within the exhibition halls, there are hardly any fundamental critics left; instead, discussions around XXL electric trucks increasingly focus on how overwhelming the market could soon become on the supplier side. We take a closer look in our post-show review.

When the IAA Transportation was still called IAA Nutzfahrzeuge, the trade fair was a major attraction for the truck and supplier industry – yet it remained curiously manageable. World regions like Europe, North America and China had little overlap. Established truck brands kept to themselves, with suppliers occasionally engaging in long-distance duels across borders. That clarity is now gone.

This is one of our key takeaways from the ongoing IAA Transportation expo in Hanover, which runs until 20 September. Around a dozen importer brands from China are showcasing their own electric trucks at the fair (and, in the case of Sinotruk, even a diesel-powered tractor unit). Trade journalists and visitors encounter names in the exhibition hall plans that they have barely heard of, let alone know how to categorise.

One thing is clear: the Chinese brands are not a homogeneous group. Behind them stand developers and manufacturers with very different industrial foundations in China – some are startups, others major corporations. What we have observed is that not all of them are yet ready to accept orders. Yes, Sany and SuperPanther are in the starting blocks, while Maxus and BYD are at least known by name. However, for most of the other new players, the priority is simply to gain visibility and establish contacts with potential partners and customers. In other words: they are displaying their electric vehicles, but in many cases, homologation is far from complete, sales structures are not yet in place, and service networks are even further behind. The Chinese brands are at the threshold of Europe's e-truck sector, but they still need time to build import capabilities, local structures and market knowledge. However, many of them bring scaling expertise – because the electric truck market in China is already significantly more mature than in Europe.

For all involved, one thing remains clear: IAA Transportation is comfortably on home turf for both German manufacturers and their counterparts in neighbouring European countries. For example, Daimler Truck is leveraging its magnetic appeal at the fair to spotlight its new electric low-floor truck. For this new version of the eActros, the developers rotated the three battery blocks by 90 degrees, squeezing out the necessary centimetres to reduce the fifth-wheel height. Meanwhile, in other halls, MAN and Scania are showcasing their new long-haul electric truck variants, for which they have installed a seventh battery pack behind the cab. The developers of both Traton Group brands apparently had free rein, as the solutions differ in terms of design but not in terms of outcome: both brands now claim up to 720 km of range "in ideal conditions" – because the batteries have received a fundamental cell upgrade and can now store more energy in the same installation space.

In short: German market leaders and other European players remain agile, developing further electric truck variants in close alignment with customer demand. Whether or not the resulting competition around range is helpful remains to be seen. Too large a gap between range and payload could unsettle potential customers. However, what we are seeing is that creative engineers in development departments are now truly coming into their own: the still-young powertrain technology, combined with rapid battery improvements, holds the potential for swift technological leaps.

The trend towards three-axle electric tractor units with a steerable trailing axle for long-haul transport is clearly recognisable: Volvo Trucks and Renault Trucks have led the way – now MAN and Scania have joined them. And we can safely assume that developers are already working on further electric variants. DAF, for example, is showcasing its versatility with electric chassis in Hanover. We are hearing that, faced with the queue of Chinese importers, the pressure to succeed is mounting. The old arrogance is gone – which leads us to another key insight: the established brands understand that they cannot afford to let up now if they want to avoid a scenario like the one that unfolded in the passenger car sector. However, investing in multiple technology strands – diesel, BEV and, in some cases, H2 combustion engines, LNG and fuel cells – requires significant effort and capital. Not everyone can keep pace. Iveco's absence from the fair speaks volumes in this regard.

Two years on from the last IAA Transportation, we can independently observe far more people at and around the fair are now casually discussing kilowatt-hours, energy content, e-machines, axle loads and payloads. Employees, suppliers and customers are increasingly realising that the numbers can add up for an electric truck (not least due to high fuel prices), but that it is not equivalent to the typical diesel all-rounder. More time must be invested in route analysis, and there are questions that need answering: "what do I actually need: range, payload, fast charging?" Awareness is growing, though courage is still lacking in some quarters – a sentiment shared by electro-trucker Tobias Wagner in the latest electrive podcast. Wagner, for his part, will set off from the fair on Sunday in a converted Mercedes electric truck to embark on an electric world tour. With this stunt, he is sure to go down as one of the faces of this year's IAA Transportation in the fair's annals.

And Tesla? The US company is staying true to its unadapted brand strategy, at least where the truck sector is concerned: the Tesla Semi for Europe has no sleeper cab, no CCS charging port – and hardly outstanding range. The impression is that Tesla does not intend to enter the mass market with its 6×4 tractor unit but instead aims to reach very specific (large) customers with a payload- and TCO-optimised electric truck. This aligns with the announcement that it plans to not only offer its own megawatt chargers for public use but also sell them directly to companies. One thing we should bear in mind ahead of next year's roll-out: of all the importers, Tesla has the most expertise in building charging networks in Europe – and already has high-voltage specialists in its car workshops. If Tesla's management is serious about its newly announced plans and the "very competitive price" it has promised, then Tesla could shake up Europe's e-truck market – at least a little faster than some Chinese players.

Tesla, alongside BYD, is also the only OEM with its own battery cell production – and is thus somewhat immune to geopolitical supply chain disruptions. All other manufacturers source the cells at the heart of their e-trucks externally. This brings us to one of the unsung rising stars of IAA Transportation: CATL is presenting a new commercial vehicle battery at the fair. The truly striking fact, however, is that a large proportion of all the electric trucks on display in Hanover cannot do without CATL: they either contain CATL cells or ready-to-install batteries from the company. This includes the Mercedes eActros series, the MAN eTGX and its smaller siblings, the electric Scania trucks and the DAF chassis showcased in Hanover. And these are just the European brands – many Chinese exhibitors also rely on CATL technology. The conclusion: when it comes to the core component of e-trucks, China is becoming almost indispensable. This may not be the best thing where the concept of a resilient, diversified supply chain is concerned.

Could hydrogen make us more independent at this point? At IAA Transportation, an alliance of Daimler Truck, Volvo Group and Toyota, together with infrastructure partners, is indeed promoting a concerted new push in this direction. However, details remain scarce, except for the plan to use prototype small series to achieve an initial scaling stage for hydrogen trucks by 2030. In conversations at the fair, the industry appears deeply divided over whether these investments will really pay off.

The German government intends to support this push but is prioritising funding for charging infrastructure: after the first funding rounds for truck chargers in depots and public locations were heavily oversubscribed in early summer, we learned at the fair that the next rounds will follow in autumn. Once again, depot chargers and public truck chargers will be subsidised. In other words, policymakers are aware that a momentum is building and should be seized to accelerate the electrification of logistics on a broad scale.

Politics is represented at the fair by the German Federal Minister of Transport Steffen Bilger and Johannes Pallasch from the National Charging Infrastructure Control Centre. The latter is symbolically celebrating the initial implementation of a truck charging network in Germany. Yet, one group of players is conspicuously absent in Hanover. Wherever you go, everyone is calling for faster grid expansion and quicker connections – but the key players responsible for delivering them are nowhere to be seen.

We therefore propose: how about giving the elusive energy sector an extra invitation in two years' time?
