# Fortum tests new battery recycling process

**Author**: Jasmin Apitzsch-Krause | **Date**: 24.11.2026 - 14:00

---

For the first time, Fortum Battery Recycling has produced customer samples of nickel sulfate, cobalt sulfate, and lithium hydroxide in battery grade using its new recycling process. The materials, obtained at the Harjavalta facility in Finland, are currently being evaluated by potential customers.

The production of battery materials took place as part of a pilot campaign at the Harjavalta site in Finland. Using the recovered materials, Fortum aims to demonstrate the potential of its new recycling technology for producing high-purity battery raw materials while reducing CO₂ emissions. The company states that feedback from ongoing customer qualification processes has been promising so far. In parallel, Fortum is in discussions with several potential buyers regarding possible offtake agreements.

“Our ability to produce these battery-grade materials for our customers marks a major step forward in advancing battery recycling and strengthening regional supply in Europe,” explained Anssi Airas, Head of Fortum Battery Recycling. Building a competitive and resilient battery value chain requires not only additional capacity but also technologies that offer a genuine competitive advantage.

According to Fortum, a key feature of the new recycling process is the internal closed-loop circulation of essential process chemicals. This is intended to reduce chemical consumption, waste generation, and CO₂ emissions. Additionally, Fortum is focusing on the electrification of industrial processes and the use of clean electricity to enable material conversion with a reduced need for externally supplied chemicals. However, the company’s current announcement does not provide specific details on recovery rates, energy consumption, or potential CO₂ savings.

The development of more efficient recycling processes is particularly relevant given the current economic challenges of battery recycling in Europe. As a study published in August under the EU research project Safeloop shows, the calculated loss currently amounts to around €1.90 euros per kilogram of battery pack. The researchers identify hazardous goods transport, battery pack disassembly, and uncertainties in material recovery as the main cost drivers. However, the study suggests that various process optimisations could reduce recycling costs by around 34 per cent.

At the same time, the supply of recycled battery raw materials is also gaining importance from a regulatory perspective: starting in August 2031, the EU Battery Regulation (DE) will mandate minimum shares of recycled materials for certain types of batteries.

With the further development of its recycling technology, Fortum also aims to strengthen Europe’s supply of battery raw materials. The project in Harjavalta was already classified as a strategic project by the European Commission in 2025 under the Critical Raw Materials Act (CRMA).

For the planned expansion of the site, Business Finland, the Finnish economic development agency, has approved funding of €86.4 million euros. In addition, Fortum Battery Recycling has signed a funding agreement with the European Executive Agency for Climate, Infrastructure and Environment (CINEA) for up to 40 million euros from the EU Innovation Fund.

Fortum’s current announcement does not specify a concrete timeline for the industrial scaling of the new recycling process. The company has also not yet disclosed the names of the potential buyers of the recycled battery materials.
