# EV Reveals Have Gotten Way, Way Too Drawn Out. The Ford Fathom Proves It

**Author**: Mack Hogan | **Date**: Aug 7, 2026

---

In August 2025, Ford announced big news to the world: It was developing an all-new electric pickup truck that would cost under $30,000 when it arrived in a couple of years. A full year later, just this week, Ford dropped another big announcement: It was still developing a midsize electric truck, now dubbed the "Fathom" and priced at—you guessed it—just under $30,000.

In the years between these two announcements, Ford has had at least three major announcements and events surrounding the project. The author even got to tour the facility where it's being developed. But we have learned precious little about the truck: It will have native Apple Maps, a standard touch screen, BlueCruise hardware, a 400-volt architecture, and more passenger space than a Toyota RAV4. It will arrive in late 2027, assuming things go to plan.

The list of things we don't know is long: the final range of either battery pack, whether there will only be two options, towing capacity, power output, all-wheel-drive pricing, or what it will look like inside or out.

This is proving out a problem that has faced the EV world for years: automakers have started announcing cars way too early, and they risk losing out on the hype when they finally arrive.

**It's Not Just Ford**

Tesla deserves credit or blame for starting this. The company pioneered revealing cars far before they were even developed, leading to the Model 3 and Cybertruck both reaching volume production far later than expected and being priced higher than initially promised.

Other automakers followed: Land Rover announced an electric Range Rover in 2023, and prototypes were driven over a year ago, yet it still isn't for sale. Rivian announced the R2, R3, and R3X in spring 2024; the R2 finally started deliveries in June, but the R3 and R3X remain distant. Scout announced the Terra and Traveler in 2024, with "initial production" on track for 2027 but customer deliveries not starting until 2028 — potentially four years after the reveal. The Tesla Roadster 2.0 was revealed nine years ago and still isn't in production.

**Why This Happens**

There's a clear audience for these multi-year public development cycles: shareholders. The original Tesla Model 3 was publicly confirmed by Elon Musk nearly four years before it went on sale, at a time when the company needed investors to see its long-term growth strategy. Teasing the Model 3 helped Tesla keep investors excited early, but its extremely long and troubled ramp-up gave the company a reputation for over-optimistic timetables — a pattern repeated with Full Self-Driving, the Cybertruck, the Semi, and the second-generation Roadster.

It's become a way for brands to claim that, while current products struggle to compete profitably, good things are right around the corner — helpful for Rivian to show a long-term plan, or for Volkswagen to say its reborn Scout truck brand will fix its US EV struggles. Mostly, it's a natural result of the attention economy: drip-feeding news yields multiple news cycles instead of just one.

**Why It's Annoying For Consumers And Dangerous For Car Companies**

When everyone does it, the tactic stops working — years of false promises have left a cloud of skepticism over every EV reveal. The Ford F-150 Lightning's price quickly climbed from its announced $40,000 base MSRP. The Volvo EX90 was late and unfinished. The Chevy Blazer EV SS faced multiple delays. The Honda 0 Series was shown in 2024 and then canceled just months before deliveries were set to begin. Porsche's 718 Boxster and Cayman EVs remain on-again-off-again.

Even hitting a target exactly means giving competitors a multi-year roadmap to beat you to market, while remaining at the mercy of pandemics, wars, and supplier issues that can make promised pricing and specs irrelevant. Automakers do it anyway because the business isn't just about selling cars — it's about staying relevant to buyers and shareholders. Tesla proved hype can excite shareholders and unlock capital, creating a feedback loop of hype, cash, better products, and more sales. But as more companies follow the playbook, customers, journalists, and investors grow increasingly skeptical of promises with fuzzy timeframes.
