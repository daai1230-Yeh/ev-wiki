# Does Ultra-Fast Charging Ruin An EV Battery? BYD Did It 350 Times In 9 Days To Find Out

**Author**: Mack Hogan | **Date**: Aug 27, 2026, 2:33pm ET

---

*The Yangwang U7 charges from 10-97% in just 9 minutes. Here's how that affects battery health.*

**Key points**

- BYD kicked off a megawatt charging war in 2025, then followed it up with even faster second-generation "Flash" charging.
- The Yangwang U7 can go from 10-97% in 9 minutes on a Flash charger.
- To prove its durability, the company did 18,000 miles at nearly 150 mph over 9 days, with 350 Flash charging stops, followed by a battery health test.

BYD kicked off a new phase of the charging war when it introduced "Megawatt Flash Charging" in 2025, offering speeds that could get a car from 10-60% in just 5 minutes. Competitors quickly responded with their own 1,000-plus-kilowatt solutions. BYD responded with a next-generation battery and charging system for its BYD, Denza, Yangwang and Fangchengbao brands. Cars equipped with the new tech can charge from 10-97% in as little as 9 minutes.

But one question has lingered: how do these ultra-quick charging speeds affect battery longevity? That's still an open question, but this week brought encouraging evidence — as with most batteries, degradation appears not to be as bad as consumers may fear.

Yangwang recently put its U7 sedan through the ringer to prove it, as CarNewsChina reported. The company says it picked a random U7 off a showroom floor — not a specially prepared factory car — and ran it at 240 kph (149 mph) on a test track in Nanning, China, for nine straight days. The car covered 30,000 km (18,600 miles) during this time and required 350 Flash charging stops, during which the car could draw up to 640 kW at a time.

That is an enormous amount of heat and charging for a battery to manage. At the end of the test, the company ran a degradation test on the U7. The result: the car still had 98.7% of its battery capacity remaining.

This is particularly encouraging because battery degradation is steepest during the beginning of a battery's life — it typically starts high before levelling out. So if the first 18,000 miles were this intense with this little degradation, it bodes well for the car's endurance later in life.

The U7 also did this during a stretch where the average temperature was 36°C (97°F). High speeds, high ambient temperatures and high charging power all stress the thermal management system of a battery, which has to stay within a certain temperature range for safety, longevity and performance. The U7's held up, maintaining high speed after days of high-speed travel and high-temperature charging events. That means it shouldn't show the sort of rapid degradation seen in cars that can't adequately control pack temperature.

But conclusions shouldn't be too rosy. Temperature and fast charging both increase degradation, but calendar degradation can be just as important — meaning it's hard to know how well a battery will hold up over time until enough time passes.

That's an inherent limitation for any new product. But tests like these prove the thermal management system is up to the task of managing high-heat charging events. As long as it continues working as designed, it shouldn't see significantly worse degradation than slower-charging cars — at least in theory.
