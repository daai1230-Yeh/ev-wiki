# Interview: Why software will define electric truck charging

**Author**: Carla Westerheide | **Date**: 30.07.2026

---

With dragonize's e-truck charging ecosystem, chargecloud positions itself as a technology partner for the next expansion stage of commercial vehicle electric mobility. In an interview, CEO Markus Bach explains why smart charging becomes mandatory for large e-truck fleets, the role software plays in load and energy management, and why the leap from pilot project to depot charging park is primarily a question of ensuring every truck is charged and ready when it is scheduled to leave.

With chargecloud's recently launched e-truck charging ecosystem with dragonize, the German charging software provider acts as the technology partner, providing the chargecloud OS as the Charge Point Management System (CPMS) for the network. This enables logistics companies to share and jointly use depot charging points.

**On the difference from passenger car charging**: For commercial vehicles, charging is part of the operational process — dependencies on deployment schedules, routes, or dwell times must be accounted for, and disruptions can significantly impact operations. A dispatcher doesn't just want to reserve a charging point for a truck, they want to secure the charging power, requiring the operating system to interact with third-party systems, including truck-specific location data such as parking dimensions and connector position.

**On scaling from pilots to large depots**: The central challenge is grid capacity — a full fleet transition typically requires new energy procurement concepts like solar PV, battery storage, or new grid connections. "The real leap doesn't come with the second or third vehicle, but when the pilot project becomes an operational business," requiring evolved processes, responsibilities, and system landscapes. At scale, standardised processes, monitoring, and clear roles become essential.

**On load management at scale**: Once charging involves 100+ vehicles at one site, smart charging becomes decisive for reliable, economical operation — distributing power so vehicles are ready on time without creating load peaks, ideally linked with route planning and dispatching.

**On data needs**: Fleet operators need three data layers — operational (deployment schedules, dwell times, priorities), vehicle (state of charge, energy requirements, charging power), and energy (connection power, load peaks, electricity contracts) — plus increasingly important forecast data.

**On customer priorities**: Four themes dominate — network charging/site sharing with direct B2B billing (not roaming, since drivers won't pay by credit card), interoperability for end-to-end operational control, cost transparency at the vehicle level (critical for TCO), and truck-specific location data.

**On MCS (Megawatt Charging System)**: From a software perspective, MCS requirements are manageable — the new connector type needs integration as a filter in apps and planning tools. The real challenge is grid connection capacity and managing the energy volumes MCS enables. In depots, 400 kW is usually sufficient since charging aligns with driver rest periods; MCS's primary use case is public charging. Faster charging (30 minutes vs 90) changes depot planning logic, requiring more dynamic, real-time reservation and slot planning.

**On grid constraints**: New grid connections are increasingly limiting large e-truck depot development. Software helps via in-depot load management and by enabling networking/sharing of grid connections between sites — through chargecloud's internal roaming hub, logistics providers can share limited connection capacity rather than each building isolated depots.

**On dynamic electricity tariffs**: Not yet a priority for e-truck fleet operators, who prioritize predictability and reliability. Dynamic energy procurement remains rare in B2B, where fixed supply contracts dominate. Currently, avoiding load peaks to reduce grid charges matters more than dynamic pricing.

**Looking ahead to 2030**: Interoperability remains the most important system function, alongside optimizing sites as part of a network rather than in isolation, cost-effective billing at scale, and strong service management for handling disruptions, delayed returns, and priority conflicts. Bach expects most charging to remain depot-based long-term, with public fast-charging and inter-depot network sharing playing a growing supporting role for longer tours.
