# Interview with Hubject CEO Christian Hahn: "The real bottleneck is siloed thinking"

**Author**: Peter Schwierz | **Date**: 2026-08-31

---

Ahead of the intercharge network conference (ICNC26) at Berlin-Tempelhof Airport, electrive interviewed Hubject CEO Christian Hahn — who joined the company in 2012 and has been CEO since 2015 — about open standards, charging silos, the V2G business case, and the unique charging requirements for electric trucks. Around 10,000 participants were expected at ICNC26 (1–3 September 2026).

**On the smart-charging bottleneck**: Charging battery-electric passenger cars and roaming interoperability have matured — a single app now works across most of Europe's public charging points, thanks to an agreed common technical foundation. What's still missing is that depth of collaboration at the smart/bidirectional charging level, where grid operators, energy traders, fleet operators and CPOs must coordinate in real time. "The technology is largely available, and regulation is also driving standardisation forward. In my view, the real bottleneck is the industry's willingness to overcome siloed thinking," Hahn said, noting companies sometimes keep customers in closed systems because it's more advantageous short-term.

**On what "open" means**: No one has to give up the customer relationship — a CPO keeps its charging points and commercial terms but becomes accessible to more eMobility service providers. What must be abandoned is lock-in, not ownership: open systems need non-proprietary standards (ISO 15118, OCPP) and non-discriminatory access, with no penalty for a partner leaving the platform or working with multiple platforms.

**On pricing transparency**: Hubject operates at the B2B infrastructure level; driver-facing prices are set by CPOs and eMobility service providers, not Hubject, so true transparency must emerge across the entire value chain.

**On ISO 15118-20**: Under AFIR, new/modernised public charging points must support ISO 15118-2 from January 2026 and ISO 15118-20 for public and private stations from January 2027, creating a shared interoperability layer. Strong interest is already emerging in the mTLS use case among commercial charging companies and electric truck operators, but smart/bidirectional charging will take longer to move from pilots to full commercial deployment. Testing and certification matter because of the mixed installed base during the transition.

**On Plug&Charge**: Adoption depends on infrastructure support and vehicles having the function enabled — manufacturers often enable it per model/market rather than across a whole range, so visible on-road adoption lags what's technically possible. Europe now has more than five million EVs with Plug&Charge (~40% market share), and it's the preferred approach for truck charging.

**On V2G**: Corporate fleets and commercial depots are the more likely frontrunners for the first truly scalable V2G business, since a single operator and controlled charging environment make the business case easier than coordinating millions of private drivers. Private passenger cars represent the largest long-term volume but will scale later, once electricity tariffs, driver behaviour and manufacturer conditions align. On cost distribution: manufacturers invest in hardware, CPOs and energy providers in backend intelligence, and drivers bear added complexity without always seeing clear benefit — grid operators and energy traders who can monetise flexibility have a strong incentive to finance the necessary intelligence, but a relevant share of that value must reach whoever provides battery capacity. Early positioning by many players for a still-unbaked V2G "pie" is normal in a young market, Hahn said, but becomes problematic when achieved through closed, proprietary systems that fragment the market before it can scale.

**On global standards**: Full global standardisation is unlikely short-term — regions will keep their own technical implementations — but architectural principles (interoperability, open standards, trustworthy authentication) can and should be shared globally.

**On truck charging**: Passenger-car roaming was built for opportunistic, flexible use; commercial fleets instead need predictable, guaranteed access based on reservations and reliability. This doesn't require a full restart, but existing roles (CPOs, eMobility service providers, platform operators) need added capabilities: reservation systems, access controls for secure sites, truck-specific site data, and authentication seamless enough to make Plug&Charge a necessity rather than a convenience. Hubject has developed a cross-provider reservation platform for truck charging as a practical example of this kind of ecosystem collaboration.
