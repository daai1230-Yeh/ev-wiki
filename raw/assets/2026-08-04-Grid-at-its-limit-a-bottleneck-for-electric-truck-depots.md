# Grid at its limit: a bottleneck for electric truck depots?

**Author**: Peter Schwierz | **Date**: 2026-08-02

---

Grid capacity is widely regarded as one of the biggest barriers to expanding charging infrastructure – particularly for electric truck depots. Whether industrial sites can accommodate additional charging capacity ultimately depends on the local distribution system operator (DSO). Electrive interviewed one to learn how the situation looks in practice.

As battery-electric truck fleets grow, grid capacity is becoming one of the biggest constraints on depot charging—not only in Germany but across Europe and other major logistics markets. Fleet operators increasingly hear the same advice: engage with the local grid operator as early as possible. Professor Markus Lienkamp of the Technical University of Munich described one of the "actually relevant questions" as: "How quickly can logistics depots secure their grid connections or adjusted power supply levels?"

That puts distribution system operators at the centre of the transition. They must accommodate growing demand from heat pumps, electric vehicle charging infrastructure, data centres and renewable energy installations while maintaining grid stability. Germany's electricity distribution network is unusually fragmented, with more than 860 distribution system operators (DSOs), making it a useful case study for the challenges of coordinating grid upgrades for transport electrification.

Netze BW, one of Germany's largest electricity distribution network operators, is currently testing grid-friendly charging for electric trucks at logistics depots through its FlexCharge BW ("Grid Lab for Electric Trucks") pilot project. The field trial involves two logistics companies in southwest Germany. Project leads Dr Kathrin Walz and Nadine Eisinger shared progress in an interview.

They are in the middle of the field trial phase, collecting real-world charging and operational data at four sites to understand charging processes in everyday logistics and develop grid-oriented charging, while also building digital tools to align grid and logistics needs. The biggest practical challenge is coordinating two very different systems — the electricity grid and logistics operations — without disrupting fleet operations.

Initial analyses show that time windows emerge between vehicle arrival, charging, and next deployment, offering scope for flexible charging control, including shifting charging to night hours or increasing power during low-demand periods, though firm conclusions await the field test beginning in September.

Charging management systems already on the market can respond to external signals such as electricity prices or route changes at short notice, but the team is working with schedules finalised by the day before to keep logistics operations reliable. Coordination complexity increases with multiple sites, more vehicles, routes, and charging points, and depends heavily on digitalisation and the local grid region.

At Spedition Stickel, an upcoming field test this autumn will investigate how favourable electricity prices, local photovoltaic generation, and grid conditions can be harmonised together for the first time.

Grid-oriented charging does not replace grid expansion, which remains a key prerequisite for electrifying freight transport — but it can help use existing grid infrastructure more efficiently. For example, not all trucks parked overnight need full-power charging immediately; vehicles not needed until morning can charge later or at adjusted power levels when the grid is less congested.

For such models to scale, DSOs need reliable forecasts of expected power demand from logistics companies, exchanged digitally and automatically, so DSOs can provide flexible power schedules aligned with grid conditions.

The overall thesis: electrification of road freight transport will not succeed through infrastructure expansion alone — the decisive factor is intelligent interaction between the power grid and logistics, with both sides aligning processes as early as possible. Some logistics companies have found they require less connection capacity than initially assumed, making charging and energy management worth considering early. The DSO's goal is combining forward-looking grid expansion with intelligent solutions that make efficient use of existing infrastructure.
