# This Study Covered 500,000 EV Batteries. Some Models Aged Much Better Than Others

**Author**: Tim Levin | **Date**: 2026-09-02

---

Worried that an EV bought today will end up like an old laptop or smartphone sitting in a drawer in a few years? A new study of 500,000 EV battery health tests over the last five years confirms that electric car batteries hold up remarkably well over tens of thousands of miles — though the car chosen and how it's used can change the picture dramatically.

The data comes from Aviloo, an EV battery diagnostics company, which compiled results from half-a-million health tests spanning 20 popular electric car models. The battery checks of used EVs occurred from 2022 to 2026 across North America, South America, Europe, Asia, and Australia.

Aviloo's state-of-health scores reflect the percentage of a battery's original usable capacity still available — an EV with an observed 95% state of health should return about 95% of its original range on a full charge. For each model, Aviloo calculated the median state of health at three milestones: 50,000 km (31,068 miles), 100,000 km (62,137 miles), and 150,000 km (93,205 miles).

After 50,000 km, the Hyundai Ioniq 5 had the most capacity remaining, with a median battery capacity over 97%. The Mercedes-Benz EQA (not sold in the U.S.) came second at 96.56%. All 20 models earned median scores over 90%. Excluding two outliers — the Renault Zoe and previous-gen Nissan Leaf, which use air-cooled batteries — all models notched a median health of roughly 94% or higher. Aviloo notes vehicles with smaller batteries tend to see faster degradation since they need more top-ups to go the same distance, and those extra charging cycles add up.

At 100,000 km, the Ioniq 5 won again with over 95% capacity remaining. Other top performers: EQA (95%), Hyundai Kona Electric (94.3%), BMW i4 (94.3%), and Ford Mustang Mach-E (93.45%). Excluding outliers, all models notched at least 90% median health.

At 150,000 km, median values clustered between 90% and 94% for most models, apart from smaller EVs like the Mini Cooper SE (87.11%), Leaf (86.67%), and Zoe (87.33%). The Ioniq 5 and EQA virtually tied for the top spot at 93.79% and 93.97% capacity respectively, with the i4 (93.62%), Kona EV (92.95%), and Volvo XC40 Recharge (92.79%) close behind.

The two Teslas in the study — Model Y and Model 3 — landed in the middle-to-bottom of the pack. After 150,000 km, Model Ys showed a median health of 90.3%, while Model 3s came in at 88.94%. The Ioniq 5 was the clear standout, winning two categories.

The report reiterates findings from other research: popular EVs tend to retain most of their range — sometimes well over 90% — even after close to 100,000 miles. Even the worst performers in this dataset still had around 87% of their battery capacity remaining after several years of use.

Results vary widely within a given model too. For the Model Y after 150,000 miles, the range spans 82.9% to 94.9%; for the i4, 87.5% to 96.9%; for the Mustang Mach-E, 85.3% to 96.1%; for the Model 3, 80.28% to 94.53%. Aviloo says hotter climates, frequent fast charging, and keeping a car topped up to 100% for hours on end can explain the spread, along with technical differences like battery chemistry and calendar age.

For EV owners and shoppers, the takeaway is mostly good news: batteries aren't turning into bricks after 100,000 miles. After about that much mileage, a roughly 10% drop in range is a reasonable expectation — though not a guarantee, since usage habits make a big difference over the long haul.
