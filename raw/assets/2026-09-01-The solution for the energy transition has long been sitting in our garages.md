# The solution for the energy transition has long been sitting in our garages

**Author**: Thomas Raffeiner, Founder & CEO of The Mobility House (guest post) | **Date**: 2026-08-31

---

Drought, volatile electricity prices, and geopolitical dependencies show how vulnerable Europe's energy supply is. For Thomas Raffeiner, an important part of the solution has long been on our roads: in the batteries of millions of electric cars.

One look at this summer is enough to see it: our energy system is vulnerable. In early August, the Danube reached a historic low. In Hungary, the Paks nuclear power plant had to temporarily reduce its output significantly because cooling water was scarce. A power plant that normally supplies almost half of Hungary's electricity was suddenly constrained by the water level of a river. In Romania, one of the two reactors at the Cernavodă nuclear power plant also had to be shut down.

At the same time, we are once again seeing how dependent Europe is on energy imports. The conflict in the Strait of Hormuz is impacting one of the most important trade routes for oil and gas. A few years ago, it was Russia. This year, it is the Persian Gulf. The crisis changes, but the dependency remains.

For me, the conclusion is clear: we must break free from fossil fuel dependencies. And not just for the sake of the climate. It also makes economic and geopolitical sense. We have wind and solar energy right here in Europe. We don't need to transport them thousands of kilometres by tanker or pipeline. And no one can turn off the tap on us.

## Our problem is increasingly one of timing

Of course, we must continue to expand wind and solar energy. But more generation alone is not enough. Even today, we regularly experience hours with abundant renewable electricity and low or even negative prices. A few hours later, the situation can look completely different. At the end of June, electricity in Germany cost around €90 per megawatt-hour at midday. By the evening, the price had soared to almost €600. Within a few hours, the price had more than sextupled. Solar production declined, but electricity demand remained high.

This is precisely the problem. We don't just need more renewable electricity. We must shift it from the hours when we have too much to the hours when we need it. To do this, we need storage and flexibility.

I am currently travelling extensively in Eastern Europe. In Bulgaria, we are working with Electrohold Trade on a pool of solar parks and battery storage systems with a capacity of around 2.9 gigawatt-hours. The storage systems are located directly at the solar parks. They absorb electricity when it is available and release it later. Such stationary large-scale storage systems will be a key part of our energy system. We need many more of them.

## Millions of batteries are already sitting idle

In Germany, more than two million electric cars are already on the roads. Or, more accurately, most of the time they are parked in driveways or garages.

Combined, their batteries already provide around 142 gigawatt-hours of storage capacity. By 2030, this could exceed 300 gigawatt-hours. In comparison, we expect stationary large-scale storage systems to reach only around 20 gigawatt-hours by then.

Of course, not every kilowatt-hour of a car battery is available to the electricity system at all times. Nor does it need to be. Even a fraction of this capacity offers enormous flexibility. We have produced these batteries. We have paid for them. Now we should use them.

This is precisely why bidirectional charging is so important to me. Vehicle-to-Home (V2H) turns the vehicle battery into a storage system for the household. Solar power from the roof can be stored in the car during the day and used in the household in the evening. This reduces grid dependency and utilises the largest battery already present in many households.

Vehicle-to-Grid (V2G) goes a step further. Multiple vehicles are pooled, and their flexibility is utilised in the electricity market and, in future, for the grid. They charge when renewable generation is high and prices are low, and can feed electricity back into the grid when it is scarce and expensive. Mobility always takes priority. For the customer, the benefit is simple: the car can earn money while it is parked.

At the same time, this creates a large decentralised storage system for the energy system. It absorbs renewable electricity, can reduce peak loads, and replaces expensive fossil fuel generation during critical hours. And we are no longer talking about research projects. In France, a commercial V2G offering for private customers has been available since 2024. In Germany, the first offerings are also available, with more to come. Vehicles, standards, and charging technology are already in place.

## Now is the time for implementation

Technology is no longer a barrier. Germany has also taken important regulatory steps. However, there is still a long way to go between passing a law and delivering a functional product to all customers.

This is particularly evident with smart meters. For V2G, we need reliable measurement data and functioning digital processes. Other European countries are much further ahead in this regard. And having more than 800 grid operators in Germany should not mean we have to implement V2G in 800 different ways.

There is still work to be done on electricity taxation, data access, and the marketing of flexibility. However, these are not insurmountable problems. The direction is right. Now we need to move faster. Of course, we will continue to expand the power grid. We need more wind and solar energy and more stationary large-scale storage. It's not an either-or situation.

But before we keep building new infrastructure, we should make better use of what is already available.

This, for me, is the decisive point about V2G. The electric car is not just an additional electricity consumer. Its battery can become part of the solution—for the household, the power grid, and the energy market.

We have already paid for a large part of the solution to the energy transition. The storage systems are in our garages and on our streets. Now we must use them.
