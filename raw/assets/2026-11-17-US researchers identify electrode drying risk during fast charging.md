# US researchers identify electrode drying risk during fast charging

**Author**: Cora Werwitzke | **Date**: 2026-11-16

---

Researchers at the University of Maryland have found that lithium ions carry solvent molecules with them as they move through a battery. During fast charging, however, the solvent may not flow back quickly enough, causing the positive electrode to dry out and slowing the charging process.

A team at the University of Maryland has made a contribution to fundamental lithium-ion battery research. The researchers have developed a new electrolyte designed to prevent electrode drying. They tested its chemical composition in a 4 Ah pouch cell, achieving an 80 per cent charge in 12.8 minutes and 90 per cent in 14.7 minutes. The findings were published in the journal Science.

The research began with the observation that lithium ions carry several molecules of a key electrolyte component – the solvent – as they move between the battery's positive and negative electrodes. "At slower charging speeds, the solvent can flow back into the porous positive electrode, replenishing what has moved away. When charging accelerates, however, that return flow struggles to keep pace. The electrode begins to dry out, and charging slows markedly," states an accompanying announcement from the researchers.

Chemists know this phenomenon as 'electro-osmotic drag', but it has primarily been observed in fuel cells. "Its role in lithium-ion batteries had been overlooked until the Maryland-led study," the university writes.

The research team also tested how the risk of electrode drying could be reduced. "By adding fluorine-containing chemical groups to solvent molecules within electrolytes, the researchers reduced the attraction between the solvent and the lithium ions, and they encouraged the solvent to flow back into the positive terminal more readily. This new chemistry drastically reduces the chances of electrodes drying out, even at fast-charging speeds," the scientists explain.

The team tested the modified electrolyte in the 4 Ah pouch cell, achieving the charging times mentioned above. The researchers now aim to reduce charging times to less than ten minutes. To do so, they plan to combine the fluorinated solvent with another chemistry-based approach centred on a self-developed 'adaptive electrolyte'. "That electrolyte separates to protect both electrodes from the stresses of fast charging," the university reports.
