# Fraunhofer explores lighter fuel cell bipolar plates

**Author**: Jasmin Apitzsch-Krause | **Date**: 2026-11-23

---

The Fraunhofer Institute for Machine Tools and Forming Technology (IWU) has developed new manufacturing processes for particularly lightweight bipolar plates made of carbon fiber-reinforced plastic. The technology could make fuel cells for drones, small aircraft, and helicopters lighter and more compact in the future.

Under the StruBiPlate research project, Fraunhofer IWU, together with partners, investigated how bipolar plates for fuel cells could be made lighter using carbon fibre-reinforced plastic (CFRP). The focus was on developing new manufacturing and structuring processes to combine the advantages of metallic and graphite bipolar plates. For aviation applications, weight and installation space are particularly critical factors.

Bipolar plates perform several functions within a fuel cell stack. They distribute reaction gases via fine channel structures, enable electrical connections between individual cells, and support thermal management. To date, metals and graphite have primarily been used for this purpose. While metallic bipolar plates are relatively thin and mechanically robust, graphite offers good electrical conductivity and chemical resistance. However, due to its mechanical properties, graphite requires greater wall thicknesses.

CFRP offers an alternative. This fibre composite material combines high strength with low weight. Additionally, its thermal properties can be tailored by adjusting the orientation of the carbon fibres and the structure of the individual material layers.

Initially, the researchers examined whether the required channel structures could be directly embossed into CFRP material. However, more than 35 individual tests showed that formability reaches its limits, particularly with channel paths running perpendicular to the fibre direction.

## Fraunhofer combines CFRP with metal

The team then developed a hybrid approach in which a flat CFRP base plate is combined with a metallic flow field. For this, the researchers cut the required channel structures from stainless steel and laminated them onto uncured CFRP. Prior laser treatment increased the roughness of the metal surface by approximately six times, improving the conditions for bonding the two materials. The subsequent lamination process succeeded with all five surface variants tested.

In parallel, Fraunhofer IWU tested the direct structuring of already cured CFRP plates using ultrashort pulse lasers. By optimising the process, the team succeeded in producing complete flow fields and reducing the average deviation from the CAD model to 0.01 millimetres.

The corrosion resistance of the bipolar plates was also investigated. A project partner coated the CFRP samples with a combined copper-nickel coating. According to Fraunhofer IWU, one of the tested samples achieved corrosion current density and corrosion potential comparable to titanium. The researchers finally produced bipolar plate demonstrators, developed matching seals, and assembled a sample stack. A leak test using nitrogen at a pressure of 1.4 bar showed no pressure drop. However, further investigations revealed that the pressure distribution within the stack needs to be analysed more precisely.

Further development steps are required before industrial production can begin. For example, laser processing of a single flow field side in the demonstrator stage still took several hours. The long-term reliable interaction of CFRP, metallic structures, coatings, and seals also needs to be demonstrated.

The StruBiPlate project, funded by the Federal Ministry of Research, Technology, and Space, was implemented in collaboration with Connova Deutschland, Sächsische Walzengravur, and the Research and Transfer Centre Leipzig at HTWK Leipzig. The hydrogen association HZwo participated as an associated partner.

According to Fraunhofer IWU, the results provide a foundation for the further development of particularly lightweight bipolar plates. Whether this will enable the weight and installation space of complete fuel cell systems to be reduced in the future remains to be seen in further studies.

Fraunhofer is also expanding its hydrogen technology research at other locations. In October, the Centre for Energy Storage and Systems ZESS in Braunschweig was opened, which, in addition to solid-state batteries, is also dedicated to advancing hydrogen technologies.
