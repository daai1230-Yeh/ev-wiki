# First drive in the new Audi Q4 e-tron: does the facelift measure up?

**Author**: Peter Schwierz | **Date**: Jul 9, 2026 (updated Jul 10, 2026)

---

The Q4 e-tron has been Audi's electric bestseller since 2021, with over 400,000 units sold to date. However, the technology had become outdated. With the 2026 facelift, Audi is making significant upgrades, particularly in the interior, and introducing two genuine innovations. During a first test drive at Lake Tegernsee, it becomes clear where the updated Q4 excels — and which question remains unanswered.

**Facts**: Battery 82 kWh (gross) | Range 531 km (WLTP) | DC charging power 165 kW | Price from €55,900 | Test car price €66,735

When Audi launched the Q4 e-tron in 2021, its mission was clear: the compact SUV was to bring electric mobility to the masses from the German carmaker in Ingolstadt. The strategy paid off. With over 350,000 units built, the Q4 e-tron, along with its coupé-like Sportback variant, has become the brand's best-selling electric vehicle. However, five years is an eternity in the world of electric mobility, and the Q4's age had become increasingly apparent — particularly in its interior and operating concept.

This is precisely where the facelift comes into play, which has been available to order since early summer. Unlike many facelifts, the focus here is not on bumpers and lights but under the sheet metal and inside the cockpit. The Q4 e-tron features a completely new screen layout, an infotainment system based on Android Automotive, a more efficient rear motor — and, as the first Audi model ever, bidirectional charging.

## The more efficient drivetrain pays off

The centrepiece of the update is the new APP350-generation PSM rear-axle motor, developed by VW, which Audi claims offers around ten per cent higher efficiency, stemming from enhancements in the motor, power electronics with silicon carbide semiconductors, software, and low-friction transmission components. In figures, the WLTP range increases by approximately 30 kilometres, depending on the variant.

On the road, this was noticeable in the tested Q4 e-tron quattro. Over 153 kilometres from Munich to Lake Tegernsee, the onboard computer recorded an average consumption of 16.4 kWh/100 km at an average speed of 57 km/h, under nearly ideal conditions (20-25°C, mostly without air conditioning, a route dominated by country roads and mountain passes with strong regenerative braking recovery). Extrapolating this to the usable battery capacity of 77 kWh, a real-world range of roughly 470 kilometres would have been achievable — not far off the 531 WLTP kilometres stated for the test vehicle. Audi specifies 22.7 kWh/100 km for motorway driving, so the more efficient Q4 is no long-distance specialist, but is more than sufficient for everyday use and occasional trips.

## Charging remains the open question

No charging stop was required during this first drive, so the review can only refer to manufacturer specifications: Audi promises a maximum DC charging power of 165 kW, with a charging time of 28 minutes for a boost from 10 to 80 per cent. In ten minutes, up to 170 kilometres of range can be added. The most powerful quattro models charge at up to 185 kW. Audi has revised the battery preconditioning system, which can now be activated automatically via the navigation destination or manually — a weak point in some MEB models until now.

## Confident and emphatically comfortable

The test vehicle's quattro system delivers 220 kW/300 PS with all-wheel drive, reaching 100 km/h in 6.2 seconds and a top speed of 180 km/h — not sports car figures, and the Q4 drives exactly as such, tuned for smoothness rather than harsh acceleration. The suspension is clearly comfort-oriented, and the regenerative braking levels can be adjusted via shift paddles. Level 2 Audi Adaptive Cruise Assist (ACA) Plus provides reliable support.

## The interior: the real leap forward

The most visible progress is in the cockpit. Audi has replaced the old architecture with the "Digital Stage," featuring an 11.9-inch digital instrument cluster and a 12.8-inch touchscreen, with an optional 12-inch passenger display. The curved display wraps around the driver in a style previously seen from Porsche, and works exceptionally well in the Q4. The new infotainment system is based on Android Automotive, complete with its own app store and an AI-powered assistant answering freely formulated questions via ChatGPT integration.

In terms of space, the Q4 leverages its MEB platform as expected — the reviewer, at 1.80m, had around 20cm of knee room in the back seat behind a similarly sized front passenger. Trade-offs must be made in boot space, which is not enormous despite the practical SUV shape.

## The unique selling point: the Q4 as a power bank

As the first Audi model, the Q4 e-tron supports bidirectional charging. Using a 230-volt socket with 16 amperes inside the cabin and an adapter solution on the rear-right charging port, the car becomes a mobile power source (Vehicle-to-Load). Vehicle-to-Home functionality will follow later, turning the Q4 into a home energy storage system. The interior socket and external adapter can be used simultaneously for a combined maximum output of 3.6 kW, available only within a battery state-of-charge window of 20-80%. "We don't want to strand our customers," an Audi spokesperson said.

## Conclusion: grown-up — but not cheap

The Q4 e-tron has always been the sensible choice among Audi's electric vehicles. With this facelift, it has also become the most refined: a comfortable, efficient, and thoroughly convincing electric SUV for everyday use, delivering realistic ranges thanks to its new drivetrain and finally featuring a cockpit that meets modern standards. The V2L function makes the Q4 significantly more versatile for active users.

It is not a bargain, though — the quattro starts at €55,900, and the well-equipped test vehicle came to €66,735. Those looking for this technology at a lower price will find it among the MEB siblings (VW ID.4, Škoda Enyaq, Cupra Born) without the Audi premium. Two questions remain unanswered: real-world charging performance across multiple fast-charging sessions, and the bigger question of whether the Q4's 400-volt MEB platform can keep pace as premium competitors and Audi's own larger PPE models move to 800 volts.
