# Munich study calls for revisions to EU CO2 regulations for cars

**Author**: Jasmin Apitzsch-Krause | **Date**: 2026-09-01

---

A study from the Technical University of Munich (TUM) is calling for changes to EU CO₂ regulations for passenger cars, arguing that EU fleet limits fail to account for emissions from production, energy provision, and recycling.

The CirculaTUM research consortium at TUM examined the climate impact of cars over their entire lifecycle, from production and operation to end-of-life treatment. Drawing on 19 previously published studies covering 47 modelled scenarios, researchers found that battery-electric vehicles produce 41 per cent fewer CO₂ emissions on average than comparable internal combustion engines over their full lifecycle, and EVs performed better in around 92 per cent of cases. However, results varied significantly: depending on vehicle and battery production, electricity mix, and recycling, emissions can be 89 per cent lower or up to 21 per cent higher compared to a combustion engine vehicle.

The researchers argue this variation highlights a weakness in the EU's current regulatory approach. CO₂ fleet limits are currently based on direct emissions during vehicle operation, meaning battery-electric vehicles are assigned a value of zero grams of CO₂ per kilometre — versus the average 41 per cent lifecycle reduction TUM found. The criticism isn't that EVs lack a climate advantage (it's present in the vast majority of scenarios), but that the regulatory assessment doesn't reflect a vehicle's full climate impact: emissions from vehicle/battery production or charging electricity aren't included in the CO2 fleet value, and savings from near-CO2-free steel, decarbonised battery production, or recycled raw materials aren't accounted for either — leaving the industry without regulatory incentive for additional CO₂ savings along the value chain.

TUM calls this a "substantial misdirection" in European climate policy and proposes a lifecycle-based standard recognising verifiable emissions reductions regardless of when they occur (production, use, or recycling), suggesting steel as a possible starting point given its relatively robust existing data foundation.

The study also questions the achievability of Europe's climate targets. Citing one of the 19 studies, TUM estimates the passenger car sector could achieve a CO₂ reduction of around 80 million tonnes by 2030 if the planned electromobility ramp-up goes to plan — versus a self-imposed target requiring around 188 million tonnes, a gap of over 100 million tonnes (this figure comes from one evaluated study, not an independent TUM forecast). Combustion engine vehicles are still expected to account for around 78 per cent of the European passenger car fleet in 2030, since rapid EV sales growth only gradually affects the overall fleet given cars' long service life.

The study lands amid political debate over future CO2 regulations: at the end of 2025, the EU Commission's Auto Package proposed reducing the CO2 reduction target for new passenger cars from 100 to 90 per cent versus 2021 starting in 2035, with remaining emissions offsettable via credits for green steel and climate-neutral fuels. TUM says this is insufficient — the credits could cover at most ten per cent of a manufacturer's required CO2 reduction — and calls for an additional mandatory, verifiable lifecycle assessment.
