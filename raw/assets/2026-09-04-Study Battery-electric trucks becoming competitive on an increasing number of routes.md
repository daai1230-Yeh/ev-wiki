# Study: Battery-electric trucks becoming competitive on an increasing number of routes

**Author**: Cora Werwitzke | **Date**: 2026-09-03

---

Today, there are many use cases where battery-electric trucks outperform their diesel counterparts in terms of total cost of ownership. By 2030, this could apply to 70 to 90 percent of road freight transport in Germany, according to a new study by the Potsdam Institute for Climate Impact Research.

The study, "Cost competitiveness of alternative heavy-duty truck technologies under real-world utilisation profiles," was published in the journal Nature by a Potsdam Institute research team. Its core message: under baseline cost and technological-maturity assumptions, battery-electric trucks (BETs) in Germany will surpass comparable diesel trucks in TCO on 70–90% of kilometres covered in heavy-duty road freight by 2030.

Unlike prior studies that often focused on average payload ranges or long-haul transport, this one evaluates the economic viability of both battery-electric and fuel cell electric trucks using microdata from four million trucks across Europe, combined with TCO forecasts under optimistic, medium, and pessimistic scenarios.

In the medium scenario, the small-battery BET variant is TCO-competitive for 91% of road freight transport by 2030, while the large-battery variant achieves this for 69% (simulating 160,000 km/year). In the optimistic scenario, nearly all usage profiles become economically viable, with TCO savings up to €0.50/km. In pessimistic scenarios, BETs only reach cost parity on 14% (small battery) and 3% (large battery) of routes.

The research team notes: "Higher annual mileages favour BETs in terms of cost-competitiveness, but are often linked to higher maximum daily mileages that can exceed the driving range under limited fast-charging availability." Accounting for maximum daily ranges and limited charging opportunities drops the medium-scenario TCO advantage to a lower bound of 21–25% of economically feasible road freight activity by 2030. However, rapid fast-charging infrastructure expansion and range improvements could raise this to 63–77% by 2035. "This analysis highlights the importance of coordinated EU-wide deployment of fast-charging infrastructure for the uptake of BETs... increased fast-charging deployment lowers the required direct driving range and allows smaller batteries to match conventional operations," the team says.

Fuel cell trucks face fewer range and operational restrictions, but their cost advantage over diesel is smaller and more uncertain due to lower efficiency and higher investment costs. In 2030, fuel cell electric trucks are cost-competitive with diesel only under the most favourable conditions, covering 79% of road-freight activity with TCO gains less than half those of battery-electric trucks. Compared directly with BETs, fuel cell trucks only become competitive under specific conditions: limited progress in BEV costs and technical parameters, persistently high electricity prices, and exceptionally low hydrogen prices — the latter requiring large-scale hydrogen imports via sea routes and later pipelines around 2030.

The researchers caution that plausible ranges for vehicle costs, technical maturity, and energy-carrier prices remain broad, and uncertainties around battery and fuel-cell-stack lifespan could affect assumptions like residual values. They note existing policy instruments such as CO2 emission standards and toll exemptions matter despite early-stage market and network barriers, and that AFIR supports rapid charging network expansion while CO2 pricing via ETS2 will further improve conditions for profitable zero-emission vehicle operation.
