# Volvo EX90: 800-volt technology makes the flagship SUV more cohesive

**Author**: Daniel Bönnighausen | **Date**: 2026-07-16

**Facts** — Model: Volvo EX90 Single Motor · Battery: 90 kWh (net) · Range: 572 km (WLTP) · DC charging power: 310 kW · Base price: €84,190

---

With the switch from a 400-volt to an 800-volt architecture in the EX90, Volvo has achieved more than just a routine model update. As a result, the flagship SUV now charges significantly faster. This has eliminated the biggest weakness of the first version. Our test drive report reveals what else this update brings and where the big Swedish car retains its distinctive characteristics.

The sequence of events is unusual: no sooner had the Volvo EX90 finally reached customers after a lengthy delay than the Swedish manufacturer unveiled its first 800-volt saloon, the ES90 – making the recently launched SUV appear slightly outdated overnight. The EX90 is now catching up. Volvo has integrated the 800-volt technology that I experienced in the ES90 into its flagship SUV. The question is whether the new architecture genuinely makes the EX90 a better vehicle or mainly highlights how quickly Volvo needed to introduce improvements.

## A flagship under time pressure

Nearly ten years after the second generation of the XC90, Volvo introduced the EX90 as its electric counterpart: built on the SPA2 platform, but initially with 400 volts. Unveiled in 2022, production was delayed until mid-2024 due to software issues. No sooner had the first vehicles been delivered than then-CEO Jim Rowan announced in March 2025 a switch to 800 volts for the next model year. Just weeks later, Volvo replaced Rowan with long-time former CEO Håkan Samuelsson, citing "rapid technological change," geopolitical uncertainties, and intensified competition. It was during this turbulent phase that the EX90 underwent its comprehensive overhaul.

With this update, Volvo has closed the charging gap and simultaneously upgraded the central computing platform. The lidar, however, has been removed. To see whether the update delivers, I tested the entry-level powertrain: the EX90 Single Motor Plus as a seven-seater.

## A land yacht in understated Volvo design

Measuring 5.04 m long, 1.96 m wide and 1.74 m high, the EX90 is an imposing yet understated SUV, with a drag coefficient of 0.29 that is respectable for a seven-seat SUV. Following its debut in the ES90, the optional electrochromatic panoramic glass roof now makes its way into the EX90, allowing it to be dimmed at the touch of a button. This addresses one of the minor everyday criticisms of the first EX90 — though the roof still cannot be opened.

## More power for the rear-wheel-drive model

The Single Motor variant features a permanent-magnet synchronous motor on the rear axle that now delivers 245 kW, matching the ES90. This enables 0–100 km/h in 6.8 seconds — output up 40 kW versus the previous Single Motor, cutting the sprint by 1.6 seconds. Kerb weight has fallen slightly to 2,585 kg (seven-seater). The Twin Motor AWD now delivers 335 kW, and the Performance version 500 kW (not currently configurable). Top speed remains Volvo-limited to 180 km/h. Maximum torque has fallen slightly from 490 to 480 Nm, but mid-range acceleration is noticeably stronger. The EX90 Single Motor remains the relaxed entry point rather than a sports car.

## Volvo knows how to build a chassis

The Single Motor does not feature air suspension (reserved for Twin Motor variants), relying on steel springs, yet ride comfort matched the 400-volt model — absorbing even cobbled streets with virtually no harsh impacts. The chassis combines double wishbones at the front, an integral-link rear axle and numerous aluminium suspension components. The steering is light and comfort-oriented with restrained feedback.

## More efficient than its reputation suggests

The test vehicle is homologated with a WLTP range of 559 km (best figure ~570 km). On a 222 km route with heavy motorway driving at 125–140 km/h, the onboard computer recorded 21 kWh/100 km; in mixed driving with a restrained style, consumption dropped to 18 kWh/100 km. This translates to a real-world range of roughly 430–500 km depending on conditions.

Volvo did not increase the battery for the update but reduced it — now 92 kWh gross / 90 kWh net (previously 101 kWh net). Calculating usable energy against WLTP range, the new Single Motor achieves ~15.7 kWh/100 km from the battery versus ~16.2–16.3 kWh/100 km before, indicating efficiency gains alongside faster charging.

## The centrepiece: 800 volts

At the same charging power, 800 volts result in lower currents, reducing losses and heat. The new EX90 Single Motor can draw up to 310 kW; a 10–80% charge should take just 22 minutes, versus 29 minutes for the predecessor. In practice, from 8 to 80% charging took around 21 minutes with a preconditioned battery, and just ten minutes to reach 50%. The charging curve peaked at 325–328 kW between 12–20%, stayed near 300 kW until 32%, then tapered to 118 kW at 80%. The AC onboard charger remains 11 kW (three-phase), so a full AC charge takes ~9 hours — a 22 kW charger is still unavailable. The EX90 is said to be hardware-ready for bidirectional charging.

## More computing power, more mature software

The EX90 was the first model based on Volvo's Superset Tech Stack, and the new model year adds significantly more computing power. New functions include Connected Safety warnings, an Emergency Stop Assist that automatically calls emergency services, night-time automatic emergency steering, and parallel parking via Park Pilot Assist. The processor upgrade is available free to existing customers during a regular workshop visit, though the 800-volt technology cannot be retrofitted. In testing, the systems felt more mature but not revolutionary, still showing weaknesses in traffic sign recognition and stop-start behaviour in jams.

## The vanished hump

The distinctive lidar hump from Luminar is gone. Volvo cites limiting "risks in the supply chain" rather than technical reasons; Luminar disagrees, is demanding compensation, and has since filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy. All-round monitoring is now handled by radar, cameras, ultrasonic sensors and the new Nvidia processors. Volvo states the EX90 and ES90 meet its safety standards even without lidar.

## Interior and practicality

The EX90 is operated almost exclusively via the 14.5-inch portrait touchscreen with Google services. The climate control distributes air pleasantly and draught-free. Drawbacks include no automatic mirror lowering and a 360-degree camera that loses reliability at close range. The interior offers exceptional front comfort and a very comfortable second row; the third row remains best for children. Boot space rises to 697 litres (from 669), plus a 49-litre frunk. Braked towing capacity increases to 1.5 tonnes (Twin Motor: 2.2 tonnes).

**Specifications** — Single Motor: RWD, 245 kW, 6.8 s, 180 km/h, 90 kWh net, 572 km WLTP, 310 kW DC, 22 min (10–80%), 11 kW AC, 697 l boot, 1.5 t towing. Twin Motor: AWD, 335 kW, 5.5 s, 103 kWh, 625 km. Twin Motor Performance: AWD, 500 kW, 4.2 s, 103 kWh, 625 km.

## Conclusion

The EX90 starts at €84,190 for the Single Motor Core; the Plus trim tested here costs €89,590, and the range tops out at the Twin Motor Performance Ultra for €106,990. This positions it alongside the BMW iX and Mercedes EQS SUV, while the Kia EV9 and Hyundai Ioniq 9 are cheaper. Does 800 volts make the EX90 a better car or mainly show how early Volvo had to make improvements? The honest answer is both. The Single Motor is slightly more powerful, more efficient, and finally charges as quickly as expected of an electric flagship in this class. Not everything is resolved — from the 11 kW AC charger to individual operating details — but overall the EX90 with 800 volts feels significantly more cohesive than before.
