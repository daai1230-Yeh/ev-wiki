# Vehicle to life: When the Kia EV5 becomes a camping battery

**Author**: electrive | **Date**: 2026-08-27

---

An electric SUV, a small caravan, and a journey to the Polish Baltic coast: The Kia EV5 had to prove itself to us not just as a tow vehicle. Thanks to Vehicle-to-Load, its 81.4-kWh battery also became a mobile power source at the campsite. And, surprisingly, the consumption with a trailer turned out to be a pleasant surprise.

For the front-wheel-drive Kia EV5 Earth with 19-inch wheels, the manufacturer states a WLTP range of 520 kilometres and a consumption of 17.1 kWh/100 km. My summer test vehicle in Ivory Silver Metallic, including optional equipment, costs EUR 54,250. Need to boil a kettle, charge a laptop, or use a hairdryer – with no power socket in sight? Not a problem. There's plenty of electricity onboard. During a stay at a small campsite near the Polish Baltic coast, it became clear that one feature of the Kia EV5 proved to be one of its greatest strengths on this trip – a feature that would typically merit only a few lines in a standard road test: vehicle-to-load.

But let's take things step by step. This is less of a conventional test of the Kia EV5 – such a review already exists on electrive. This is more of a travelogue. Nearly three weeks of real-world experience instead of test tracks. First Berlin and Brandenburg, then putting a small caravan in tow, and off via the Feldberger Seenlandschaft of lakes and forests and up to the German coastal island of Usedom, and West Pomerania to near Leba on the Polish Baltic coast, where mighty shifting dunes await.

The companion in tow is my nifty Mink Camper 2.0. A small teardrop caravan with a tare weight of 490 kilograms and a maximum permissible weight of 750 kilograms. In other words, it's pretty much the opposite of those massive white caravans that occasionally hide entire families along with half their household goods on the motorway.

Before the Mink can be hitched to the Kia, a few days of normal use are on the agenda. Here, the EV5 initially feels large. Not just objectively, with its 4.61 metres in length and 1.875-metre width, but also subjectively from the driver's seat. The bonnet stretches imposingly forward, its outer edges are not easily visible, and manoeuvring into Berlin's tight parking spaces requires some getting used to.

Visually, the Kia doesn't hide its size either. The upright front, largely enclosed grille, and striking light signature make it appear quite imposing. Since the EV5 isn't yet a common sight on every street corner, it occasionally draws curious glances.

Berlin, however, isn't its ideal setting. Even finding a parking space is simply more enjoyable with a smaller car.

As soon as you leave Berlin behind and head into Brandenburg, it's a different story. In the Ostprignitz-Ruppin district, there are plenty of narrow roads, poor road edges, and tracks where you sometimes have to pull over onto the gravel with two wheels to make way for oncoming traffic. This is where the large Kia suddenly feels just right. With 167 millimetres of ground clearance, a high seating position, and a comfortable suspension, you won't flinch even on a farm track.

The suspension is clearly tuned for comfort. It's a bit soft, feels like it sways. If you've recently sat in a current BMW SUV, you'll know what precise and refined body control feels like. The EV5 takes a more relaxed approach. It's not sporty. The body roll in corners is noticeable. But for the task ahead, it does the job nicely.

Because now a small caravan is being attached.

The front-wheel-drive model is approved to tow braked trailers up to 1,200 kilograms. That's more than enough for the small Mink. Kia's FWD model delivers 160 kW or 218 PS and a maximum of 295 Nm to the front axle. On paper, these aren't exactly high-performance figures – but combined with the lightweight camper, they're more than sufficient.

The beauty of electric drive becomes apparent in the first few kilometres. No gear shifts, no revving, no build-up. The EV5 simply pulls the Mink away. Smoothly, quietly, and entirely unremarkably. Exactly as it should be.

After a few kilometres, you almost forget there's anything hitched at all. The compact camper remains stable behind the Kia and sits neatly in its slipstream due to its dimensions. Even on rougher tracks, the combination works exceptionally well.

Only on dusty or sandy surfaces does the front-wheel-drive model remind you that 295 Nm must be transmitted solely through the front wheels. Under heavy acceleration, wheel spin can occur. If you plan to regularly tackle wet campsite grass, gravel, or rough tracks with your EV5, the all-wheel-drive model might be worth considering.

The first major joint stop is the 3000Grad Festival among the forests and lakes of the Feldberger Seenlandschaft, in the southeast of the Mecklenburgische Seenplatte lake district. Once the little caravan is on the grass, with the Kia parked next to it, the small base camp is ready. Luggage and clothes go into the electric car, while sleeping happens in the Mink.

And this is where the big power bank comes into play.

From the Earth trim level upwards, the EV5 comes with vehicle-to-load (V2L) as standard, allowing it to supply external devices with up to 3.6 kW. This includes a 230-volt socket in the boot and an adapter for the charging port.

At the festival, this is a nice feature. On the rest of the journey, it becomes truly practical.

From the Feldberger Seenlandschaft, the route first leads to Kamp am Peenestrom, opposite the island of Usedom. At a small waterside rest area, we could have used a power connection. But why bother? The Mink has its own battery and solar panel for lights and smaller 12-volt devices. Whenever more power is needed, an 81.4-kWh battery is ready and waiting.

So, plug the V2L adapter into the Kia, connect an extension lead to the camper – and suddenly, the electric car becomes a generously sized camping power bank.

The next day, the journey continues via Usedom and Swinoujscie to Poland, and later to Smoldzinski Las near the Baltic coast. We stay for four days at a small campsite. Again, the Mink isn't parked right next to a power socket. Again, it doesn't matter. Press a button on the V2L adapter: power on. Fill the kettle, charge the laptop, use the hairdryer. Power off. Done.

The kettle, in particular, becomes a favourite appliance. Anyone who has ever tried to boil a large pot of water for pasta on a small camping gas stove knows why. Preheat the water electrically, then finish it off briefly on the gas stove – perfect. The next evolutionary step is already planned with a small electric hotplate. The gas stove can then serve as a backup.

This gives V2L a completely new meaning beyond what's on the spec sheet. Sure, you can use 3.6 kW to charge an e-bike or power tools somewhere. But when camping, the function simply means you can do without a traditional grid power connection.

Unfortunately, I didn't measure how many kilowatt-hours actually flowed from the car into everyday camping life during those days. This trip wasn't suitable for a scientific V2L test. But for a practical impression, it certainly was.

The more pressing question, however, is: how much range does a caravan actually cost? Because caravans and electric cars aren't exactly natural allies. Additional weight plays a role, but aerodynamics are the main issue. A tall caravan can significantly increase the energy consumption of an electric car on the motorway.

The Mink has an unfair advantage here: it's low, compact, and at least partially disappears behind the large frontal area of the EV5. At the same time, towing a trailer naturally encourages a more moderate travelling speed.

And lo and behold, the big range shock never materialises on our trip.

On the first well-documented long motorway leg in Poland, the onboard computer shows 24.4 kWh/100 km after 192 kilometres. This is followed by 38 kilometres of brisk country road at 26.0 kWh/100 km. On another 174-kilometre leg back towards Germany, with around 22 degrees C and a slight headwind, consumption is 24.1 kWh/100 km.

Over these 404 kilometres in total, the average consumption is around 24.4 kWh/100 km. That's remarkably low. Especially since Kia itself states a WLTP motorway consumption of 23.3 kWh/100 km for the EV5 Earth with 19-inch wheels. The comparison isn't entirely fair, of course. When towing a trailer, you travel significantly slower than during a solo motorway drive. But that's precisely the point in practice.

At 24.4 kWh/100 km, the nominal 81.4 kWh battery capacity would theoretically allow for over 330 kilometres if you were to completely drain the battery. Of course, no one does that. Our realistic stages were more in the range of 250 to 300 kilometres – which is pretty much the point where the driver, passenger, or camper could use a break anyway. For a combination of an electric SUV weighing over two tonnes and a caravan, that's perfectly manageable!

Especially since the EV5 doesn't aim to break any records when it comes to fast charging. Its 400-volt architecture achieves a maximum of 150 kW, and Kia states a charging time of 30 minutes for 10 to 80 per cent. In an era of 800-volt systems with over 250 or even 300 kW, that doesn't sound particularly impressive. However, on our trip, it's surprisingly unproblematic.

The bigger issue is individual charging stations. In Ploty, the first fast charger we approach is under maintenance, and the one next to it is occupied. Since there's still enough charge in the battery, we move on to a shopping centre. There, an older charging station refuses to cooperate, neither via ad-hoc payment nor its card terminal. In the end, a German charging card via roaming does the trick. More expensive, but at least the electricity flows.

Later in Leba, the situation is similar: individual charging points instead of large charging parks, and ad-hoc payment systems with a mind of their own. Again, the pre-loaded charging card saves the day. Real-world experience occasionally trumps the pretty infrastructure maps.

At least the EV5 has a small detail that proves exceptionally clever for towing: the charging port is located at the front right, behind the front wheel. In everyday use, this takes some getting used to. But with a camper in tow, it can be a game-changer. If the charging station is at the front of the parking space, you simply drive forward, charge, and then reverse out. No need to unhitch the trailer – provided there's enough space around. We particularly appreciate charging parks at supermarkets, where there's usually enough room to manoeuvre or dodge other road users. A charging socket at the rear would be far less practical in this scenario.

Kia has also put thought into the software side of towing. As soon as the Mink is connected, the EV5 switches to trailer mode and factors the trailer into its range prediction. Additionally, you can specify whether a light, medium, or heavy trailer is being towed.

This works reliably on the trip. The displayed remaining range quickly adjusts to the significantly higher consumption – and reduces some of the range anxiety for the combination.

It's all the more curious that the detailed consumption values during the journey aren't particularly easy to find. A clear trip consumption display, like the one I know from other vehicles, simply doesn't stand out in the EV5. There's only a detailed view in the central 12.3-inch display behind the steering wheel, which you first have to locate. After a longer search on the central touchscreen, you can find a bar chart sorted by days and trips. That's where I later retrieved the relevant consumption data for this report.

Overall, the cockpit is an odd mix of digital and very analogue. There are plenty of displays, but some information is a bit awkwardly placed. The right side of the central touchscreen is barely reachable without leaning forward, and part of the climate control is hidden behind the steering wheel from the driver's perspective. The touch-sensitive areas below the display can also be accidentally triggered when resting your hand.

At the same time, almost everything still has a button or lever. Boot, charging flap, parking brake, gear selection – Kia clearly doesn't subscribe to the "one screen and nothing else" school of thought. I generally prefer this approach. But the plastic cockpit sometimes feels less modern than the spectacularly designed bodywork outside.

On long stretches – in our case, also when following trucks for extended periods – the driver assistance systems come into focus. And not everything is relaxing.

The attention warning system sometimes wants to send the driver for a break just after setting off – and it's surprisingly persistent. I don't know what my eyes did to it.

I also don't quite warm to the lane-keeping assist on some of Poland's narrow roads. The system strongly orientates itself towards the right-hand markings and tries to maintain a distance from them – even when oncoming traffic is approaching. You end up feeling like you're steering against the car, even though the assistant is supposed to be doing the work.

On the motorway, the situation improves. There, the EV5 drives calmly, quietly, and pleasantly unflustered. Even with a camper in tow. The driving modes Eco, Normal, and Sport slightly alter the character, though the name "Sport" might raise expectations that a large, softly sprung front-wheel-drive SUV doesn't intend to meet – and doesn't need to. The EV5 is a travel car. One that prefers to glide rather than carve through corners. And with the Mink in tow, that's a pretty good fit.

The interior, on the other hand, offers plenty of space. The EV5 has five fully usable seats and an official boot capacity of 566 litres; with the rear seats folded, this increases to up to 1,650 litres. There's also a frunk.

Still, the boot doesn't feel as massive as the angular bodywork might suggest. The double loading floor takes up quite a bit of height. On the plus side, small items, cables, or camping accessories disappear underneath, and the actual loading floor remains almost level.

On this trip, the Kia serves as a rolling storage unit. Sleeping happens in the Mink, while everything else ends up in the EV5. And as any camper knows, that adds up to a surprising amount of stuff.

At the end of this trip, it's not the EV5's charging performance that sticks in the mind. Nor its acceleration. And certainly not its top speed.

It's the combination of three rather unspectacular features: a large battery, a reasonable towing capacity, and vehicle-to-load.

The EV5 effortlessly tows a small camper, offers realistic travel stages of 250 to 300 kilometres despite the trailer, and consumes around 24 to 26 kWh/100 km on our most meaningful routes with the Mink. That's far less dramatic than one might expect from the phrase "electric car plus caravan." Of course, the small and aerodynamically favourable Mink helps. With a family caravan standing two and a half metres tall, this story might look very different.

At any rate, the electric drive opens up new possibilities for camping that a combustion-engine SUV simply can't offer. The 81.4-kWh battery isn't just an energy store for driving; it can also serve as a power socket for life beside the car. Boil water, charge a laptop, dry your hair – and soon, perhaps, cook entirely electrically. Vehicle-to-life instead of vehicle-to-load.

Perhaps that's the more interesting insight from this trip: when it comes to electric cars with campers, we shouldn't just talk about how much extra electricity the trailer consumes. But also about what you can do with the electricity in the car once you've arrived.
