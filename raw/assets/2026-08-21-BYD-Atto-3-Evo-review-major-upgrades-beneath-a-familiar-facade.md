# BYD Atto 3 Evo review: major upgrades beneath a familiar facade

**Author**: Kasimir Hupe | **Date**: 20.08.2026

---

## Facts

| | |
|---|---|
| Model | BYD Atto 3 Evo Design RWD |
| Battery (Net) | 74.8 kWh BYD Blade Battery (LFP) |
| Power | 230 kW (313 hp) |
| Range (WLTP) | 510 km |
| DC Charging Power | 220 kW peak |
| Base Price | 44,990 euros |

The Atto 3 Evo may look similar to its predecessor, but beneath the familiar exterior, practically everything has changed – with BYD addressing some of the Atto 3's biggest weaknesses. Instead of just 110 kW, it now charges at up to 220 kW, the battery grows to 74.8 kWh, and the drivetrain has moved from the front to the rear axle. After extensively testing the Atto 3 Evo for two weeks, here are our findings.

Some facelifts require you to park two cars side by side in order to spot the differences. Others, however, conceal an entirely new vehicle beneath practically unchanged sheet metal. The BYD Atto 3 Evo clearly falls into the second category.

Its predecessor, the Atto 3, had two major weaknesses. It had maximum charging power of 110 kW – already subpar in 2023 – and featured a diminutive 60.5 kWh battery that drained quickly on the motorway. Everything else about the model was competitive: space, build quality, equipment, and price.

BYD openly acknowledged these weaknesses and has since addressed them directly with the Evo. The result: a 74.8 kWh battery, 220 kW charging power, and a drivetrain that has shifted from the front to the rear axle. On paper, this represents a significant leap forward. On the road, it delivers – across most driving scenarios.

### Design and interior

The BYD Atto 3 Evo's dimensions remain unaltered: 4.46 m long, 1.88 m wide, 1.62 m tall, with a 2.72 m wheelbase. External updates include redesigned bumpers, slimmer side skirts, a flatter rear spoiler, and a new 18-inch wheel pattern. One change adds real everyday value: the charging port is now at the rear instead of the front right. The Atto 3 platform was awarded 5/5 stars by Euro NCAP.

The most visible improvements lie in the interior. The gear selector has moved to the steering column, streamlining the centre console and freeing space for a 50-watt actively cooled inductive charging pad plus a second phone tray, with two USB-C ports (18 W and 60 W) below. Material quality is significantly improved — soft-touch surfaces, artificial leather, contrast stitching and microfibre in frequently touched areas. The unusual door handles take about three days to get used to.

### Storage

The boot offers 490 litres (50 more than the predecessor), rising to 1,360 litres with the 40:60 rear seats folded. A 101-litre frunk is ideal for charging cables. Cell-to-body construction gives a completely flat rear floor, improving the middle seat; legroom tightens for those over 1.85 m.

### Controls

A 15.6-inch touchscreen dominates the centre with an 8.8-inch digital cluster behind the wheel. Infotainment uses integrated Google services (Maps, Play Store, Assistant), plus BYD's own "Hi BYD" cloud voice assistant. Crucially, air vents use physical rotary controls, and physical buttons remain for driving mode, hazards, Auto Hold and volume. A steering-wheel button activates the 360-degree camera view instantly.

### Driver assistance

Both variants include adaptive and intelligent cruise control, blind-spot monitoring, front and rear cross-traffic alert, emergency brake assist, lane-keeping assist and door-opening warning. Lane-keeping works flawlessly; longitudinal guidance can be jerky after overtaking. The most obvious weak point is traffic sign recognition, which does not reliably detect speed limits — likely a fixable software issue.

### Drivetrain

The switch from front- to rear-wheel drive is the most technically bold decision. A permanently excited synchronous motor at the rear delivers 230 kW and 380 Nm, giving 0–100 km/h in 5.5 seconds — faster than the latest Golf GTI (5.9 s). BYD states 180 km/h top speed for the Design, but the test car reached 205 km/h indicated. Regenerative braking has only two levels and no one-pedal driving — an unusual omission for this model year. Towing capacity is 1,500 kg braked / 750 kg unbraked.

### Driving dynamics

Rear-wheel drive is a real gain: no torque steer out of corners, no front-wheel tyre scrub on wet roads. The rear axle is now five-link (previously four-link); the front retains MacPherson struts. The suspension is tuned firmly — secure on smooth tarmac, harsh on poor country roads and cobblestones. The steering wheel vibrations noted in the April first drive did not appear over two weeks, suggesting an individual wheel imbalance. Wind noise from the mirrors and A-pillar increases noticeably beyond 130 km/h.

### Consumption and range

Test consumption averaged 17.7 kWh/100 km across all profiles (WLTP claim: 16.4). City driving achieved around 15.2 kWh; motorway cruising around 22 kWh. Best real-world range was 485 km; at the test average the 74.8 kWh battery delivers around 420 km, or about 340 km for mostly-motorway driving.

### Charging: the biggest improvement

The old Atto 3 charged at a maximum 110 kW, taking about 44 minutes for 10–80%. The Evo is rated at 220 kW and 25 minutes. In testing, peak briefly reached 225 kW, with 10–80% in about 23–24 minutes at an average of 130 kW. BYD refers to an 800-volt architecture though nominal battery voltage is 499.2 V. The battery can be preconditioned manually and automatically. AC charging remains 11 kW three-phase (about eight hours full). V2L is standard at 3 kW.

In May, BYD unveiled the third-generation Atto 3 in China (Yuan Plus), based on e-Platform 3.0 Evo with second-generation Blade Battery and Flash Charging claiming 10–70% in about five minutes — not reaching Europe until at least 2027.

### Equipment and warranty

Both trims come with heated and ventilated front seats, heated steering wheel, six-way electric front seat adjustment, dual-zone climate control, heat pump, PM2.5 filter, ambient lighting, seven airbags and eight speakers. Excellence adds head-up display, heated rear seats and panoramic roof. Warranty: six years/150,000 km vehicle, eight years/250,000 km battery (70% minimum remaining capacity), eight years/150,000 km electric drivetrain, twelve years against rust perforation.

### Pricing and competition

Officially €44,990 (Design) and €50,990 (Excellence). BYD currently offers a €9,000 manufacturer discount on the Design, bringing the real starting price to €35,990; with the maximum €6,000 government grant the potential final price is €29,990. The German discount campaign runs until 30 September 2026.

Competitors: the VW ID.3 Neo (79 kWh, 210 kW) starts at €44,995 and charges at max 183 kW. The Xpeng L03 RWD Long Range costs €38,600 with a 71.2 kWh battery, 180 kW, up to 520 km WLTP and 236 kW charging (10–80% in 20 minutes) — but only a 367-litre boot. The Leapmotor C10 RWD offers 81.9 kWh net, 220 kW and 510 km for €41,900, charging at max 180 kW.

### Conclusion

The Atto 3 Evo is more than a cosmetic facelift — it addresses precisely the two weaknesses that held its predecessor back. The jump from 110 to a measured 225 kW peak charging power and the reduction from 44 to 23 minutes is immediately noticeable in everyday use. The switch to rear-wheel drive improves driving behaviour, and 17.7 kWh average consumption is reasonable for the performance.

Criticisms: unreliable traffic sign recognition, no one-pedal driving, somewhat firm suspension, and pronounced wind noise above 130 km/h.
