# Is bidirectional AC charging nearing the mass market?

**Author**: Peter Schwierz | **Date**: 25.08.2026

---

Bidirectional charging works – and the first DC solutions are already making the leap into practice. But for the mass market, Cubos, Compleo, and Valeo are focusing on AC. The vision: Millions of affordable charging points connecting EVs with the energy system.

Bidirectional charging has been discussed for years, but it is now starting to become a reality. Electric vehicles can not only draw electricity from the grid but also feed it back. The first commercial products are reaching the market, pilot projects are growing in scale, and companies are beginning to integrate vehicle batteries into the energy market. Corporate fleets, with their long and predictable downtimes, are considered particularly well suited to providing and marketing this flexibility.

At the same time, this electrive-themed week has shown why a broader breakthrough has yet to happen. Standards, grid codes and regulatory frameworks are still not fully aligned. Even implementing ISO 15118-20 does not automatically make a charging point capable of bidirectional operation. In Germany, questions around grid fees and market integration also remain unresolved, while the highly fragmented grid operator landscape adds another layer of complexity.

The key question, then, is no longer whether bidirectional charging works, but how it can become a mass-market solution.

There are good reasons why many of today's prominent vehicle-to-grid (V2G) projects use direct current (DC). With a DC solution, the direct current from the vehicle battery is converted into grid-compliant alternating current (AC) outside the vehicle. Much of the necessary power electronics sits in the wallbox, making it easier to integrate existing vehicles and handle the complex grid connection outside the vehicle. The downside is cost: these systems are comparatively expensive.

Thomas Basbous, Director of Energy Business at Valeo Service Smart Mobility, sums up the dilemma succinctly. DC is initially the more straightforward technical route, as much of the complexity sits in the charging equipment. However, as Basbous puts it: "DC is very, very expensive, and we cannot imagine it being adopted by the mass market." Valeo is therefore focusing on a bidirectional AC wallbox, which it is initially launching with Nissan in the UK, with certification underway for Germany and France.

Christian Rühe, Chief Technology Officer at Cubos, makes a similar argument: "Because I believe this is the only way to address the mass market." If the inverter is already installed in high volumes in the vehicle, the power electronics can be scaled there, letting the wallbox stay simpler and more cost-effective. Cubos has developed a modular prototype where a unidirectional module installed today could later be replaced by a bidirectional one.

Björn Dietrich, CEO of Compleo Charging Solutions, is taking a similar approach, focusing its hardware portfolio on AC charging with a new modular platform expected to become fully bidirectional. For Dietrich, cost isn't the only argument for AC — the sheer number of existing AC charging points matters, since most infrastructure sits where cars park for long periods (home or work), which is exactly where V2G value is created.

Valeo's long-term goal is to price an AC-V2G solution at roughly the same level as standard AC charging today, with no fundamental price premium expected once volumes scale. Compleo draws a comparison to 4K televisions: bidirectionality could become a standard feature even before every use case exists for it.

However, the market isn't there yet. Dietrich warns against assuming complete investment security from the "BiDi-ready" label; Compleo's gradual rollout is scheduled to begin at the end of 2027, pending clarity on communication, grid codes, and certification requirements. Compleo and Cubos differ in approach — full certification before rollout versus replaceable modules — but share the same goal: infrastructure installed today shouldn't become an obstacle as AC-V2G matures.

Beyond hardware, the business model matters most. Valeo describes an ecosystem of vehicle, charging hardware, aggregator and energy supplier working together. Compleo is "planning products for use cases and business models that do not yet exist at this scale," per Dietrich, while open questions remain on how owners will earn money from their batteries and how cycling affects warranties. Cubos, meanwhile, is looking beyond the charging point toward a broader energy system incorporating photovoltaics, stationary storage, and dynamic tariffs.

AC could open the door to the next stage of scaling: millions of comparatively affordable charging points where vehicles remain parked for hours, with power electronics shifting into the vehicle and the wallbox becoming the link between car, building, and energy system. As Rühe puts it: "At the end of the day, it must pay off for the customer. They must save money with it."

If AC-V2G becomes a standard feature in everyday wallboxes and electric cars, bidirectional charging could shed its niche status — the car simply charging when electricity is cheapest and feeding energy back automatically when needed, with a financial benefit for its owner.
